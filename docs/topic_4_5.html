Topic 4.5

From tasks to competence

The six-layer conceptual model of competence

Final Report · §7.3, Understanding the relevance of competence and its implications

The analysis of the ATCO task taxonomy shows how automation may affect individual tasks in the future, and how those changes transfer to competency requirements, to the extent that new competencies may need to be introduced. Before moving forward with the future-proofing analysis, we establish a layer model to fully understand how competences fit into the ATCO training and licensing logic.

For this study, ATCO competence development and recognition can be understood as a layered process linking an individual's underlying potential to the formal privileges ultimately granted through qualification and licensing. The model distinguishes between the characteristics that influence a person's capacity to learn, the learning process itself, the knowledge, skills and attitudes developed through training and experience, their integration into competence, the observable performance through which competence is demonstrated, and the formal recognition of that performance through qualification or licensing. The individual layers are closely related, but they should not be treated as interchangeable.

Underlying attributes and aptitudes are the model’s starting point. These are relatively fundamental characteristics that influence whether, and how effectively, an individual is able to acquire and subsequently exercise ATCO competencies. They represent potential rather than trained competence. Relevant attributes may include cognitive abilities such as reasoning, working memory, attention and information-processing capacity; perceptual abilities such as visual perception, spatial orientation and perceptual speed; attentional control and the ability to manage several sources of information simultaneously; and broader learning capacity or trainability. Relatively stable individual characteristics, including elements such as stress tolerance, may also be relevant. In practice, this layer is addressed primarily through recruitment and selection, where the objective is to assess whether an applicant has the underlying attributes needed to develop the required competencies through training. EUROCONTROL's FEAST selection framework, for example, assesses abilities including decision-making, logical reasoning, visual perception, memory, attention, multitasking and spatial orientation and may also incorporate a personality questionnaire.

Learning and training constitute the developmental process through which this underlying potential is transformed into job-relevant knowledge, skills and attitudes, and ultimately into operational competence. Unlike the other layers, training is not a characteristic of the individual but the process through which competence is developed. It may include theoretical instruction, guided and part-task practice, simulation, on-the-job or live operational training, coaching and feedback, self-directed learning, reflection and formative assessment. The same logic applies later in an ATCO's career through continuation, conversion and recurrent training. From a competency-based perspective, the purpose of training is therefore not simply to expose the trainee to prescribed content or to complete a defined number of hours, but to progressively develop and integrate the elements required for competent performance. ICAO Doc 10056 provides the ATCO-specific methodological framework for competency-based training and assessment, within the wider competency-based training principles established by ICAO PANS-TRG.

The immediate outputs of learning can be expressed in terms of knowledge, skills and attitudes (KSA). These are the resources that an individual acquires and draws upon when performing operational tasks, but they do not in themselves constitute competence. Knowledge may include factual and conceptual knowledge, procedures, system knowledge, operational knowledge and knowledge of the local operating environment. Skills may include cognitive and perceptual skills, procedural and technical skills, HMI and system-operation skills, communication, coordination and interpersonal skills. Attitudes concern the manner in which these capabilities are applied, including safety orientation, professionalism, responsibility, willingness to cooperate, openness to feedback, adaptability and commitment to continued development. Competent performance requires mobilising these elements together rather than applying them in isolation. This distinction is consistent with the EU CBTA definition of competency, which explicitly refers to behaviours that mobilise the relevant knowledge, skills and attitudes in carrying out activities or tasks under specified conditions.

Competence represents the integrated capability to perform the activities required by the job successfully. It is therefore more than possessing relevant knowledge or being proficient in a particular skill. A competent ATCO must be able to select, combine and apply the appropriate knowledge, skills and attitudes in response to the task and the conditions in which it is performed. Competence answers the question of what the individual is capable of doing. The EU CBTA framework defines competency as a dimension of human performance that predicts successful job performance and is manifested through observable behaviours that mobilise relevant knowledge, skills and attitudes.

Performance is the observable demonstration of competence. Whereas competence describes an integrated capability, performance provides the evidence from which achievement of that capability can be assessed. It must therefore be considered in relation to the task being performed and the operational and technical conditions under which the performance is observed. Relevant evidence includes observable behaviours, consistency of performance, the integration of different competencies and the operational outcome achieved. Under the EU CBTA framework, an assessment determines whether a person meets the required performance level under given conditions by collecting evidence from observable behaviours, while performance criteria combine an observable behaviour, one or more conditions and a competency standard. This establishes a direct relationship between competence, observable behaviour, conditions and the performance standard against which competence is assessed.

The final layer, qualification and licensing, represents the formal recognition that specified requirements have been met and that the individual is authorised to exercise defined privileges. This is a regulatory or administrative status rather than another human capability. Under the present European ATCO licensing framework, formal recognition is expressed through the ATCO or student ATCO licence and, as applicable, ratings, rating endorsements, unit endorsements, language endorsements and instructor or assessor endorsements. Medical fitness constitutes a separate prerequisite for the exercise of operational privileges and should therefore not be conflated with competence itself. The licensing layer therefore answers a different question from the preceding stages: not what the individual knows or can do, but which capabilities and qualifications have been formally recognised and which privileges the individual is authorised to exercise.

Taken together, the model illustrates a progression from potential, through development and acquisition, to integrated capability, observable demonstration, and finally formal recognition. It is particularly useful for the present study because it allows recruitment, training, competency development, assessment and licensing to be considered as related but distinct elements of the ATCO qualification pathway. It also provides a basis for examining whether future licensing arrangements could recognise competence more explicitly, rather than relying primarily on the completion of predefined training pathways or unit-specific qualification structures.
