Future operational concepts

Final Report · §7.1, Future operational and technical changes

Trajectory-based operations progressively replace a succession of locally managed tactical interventions with the collaborative management of shared, continuously updated four-dimensional flight trajectories. Airborne, ground and network systems exchange trajectory intentions, constraints and predictions to support a more consistent gate-to-gate picture. For ATCO tasks, this may shift effort away from repeatedly reconstructing the likely flight path and towards assessing proposed trajectory changes, managing deviations and intervening where predictions or constraints require it. Automated trajectory prediction, conformance monitoring and synchronisation are essential enablers.

Advanced Flexible Use of Airspace (A-FUA) and Dynamic Airspace Configuration (DAC) aim to make the allocation and use of airspace more responsive to civil and military needs. Real-time exchange of airspace status, variable-profile reservations and collaborative planning tools allow configurations to change closer to the time of operation and, ultimately, across borders. Rather than working predominantly with static airspace allocations, ATCOs may need to interpret continuously updated availability and configuration information, accommodate changes in traffic planning and coordinate through increasingly system-supported processes. Here, automation primarily supports information exchange, constraint management and dynamic coordination.

SESAR uses the term “lateral and vertical complex CPDLC clearances” for advanced data-link procedures capable of communicating multi-element trajectory changes and constraints, potentially further in advance than conventional tactical voice clearances. Ground automation, downlinked flight-management-system trajectory information, and inter-centre coordination help controllers evaluate and transmit changes that affect larger portions of a flight. This may replace some sequences of discrete verbal instructions with the review, authorisation and monitoring of a more integrated clearance and associated trajectory. The exact distribution of tasks remains dependent on operational procedures and the level of automation implemented.

Remote aerodrome air traffic services enable tower services to be delivered without direct visual observation of the aerodrome, using camera-based views, surveillance sources, communications and digital information overlays. Multiple remote tower concepts extend this arrangement to services for more than one aerodrome from a shared centre or module. ATCO tasks may consequently rely more heavily on digitally mediated observation, sensor fusion, automated alerts, prioritisation and switching between operating contexts. In these concepts, automation chiefly changes how the controller acquires and organises operational information; it should not be conflated with autonomous provision of aerodrome control.

FINEST

Intermediate Report · §6.1, FINEST

In European Air Traffic Management, the transition to dynamic airspace management presents significant operational and safety implications. With air traffic expected to grow significantly over the next decade, the demand for more flexible and responsive airspace management is increasingly urgent. Recent developments indicate a clear movement away from static, geographically defined structures towards dynamic, performance-driven concepts. SESAR research and strategic planning documents, particularly those addressing Dynamic Airspace Configuration and Fully Dynamic and Optimised Airspace, outline environments in which airspace structures, sector boundaries and service responsibilities adapt continuously to traffic demand, network status and system performance objectives. This shift marks a fundamental departure from previous models, as SESAR Solution PJ09-W2-44 reconceptualises airspace as a flexible construct shaped by operational needs, thereby challenging traditional assumptions regarding the organisation and delivery of air traffic services.

Within Europe, SESAR concepts are moving from theoretical frameworks to practical implementation. Cross-border service provision initiatives are beginning to incorporate elements of dynamic airspace and flexible service responsibility. A notable example is the bilateral cooperation between Estonia and Finland, referred to as the FINEST project. Publicly available sources indicate that this initiative facilitates dynamic cross-border air traffic service provision between the two Area Control Centres by employing harmonised procedures, common rostering principles and integrated technical systems.

The move towards dynamic, integrated service provision exposes fundamental limitations in traditional Air Traffic Controller (ATCO) training and lifecycle models. Historically, training, validation and licensing have relied on stable sector geometries, predictable traffic patterns and clearly defined areas of responsibility. SESAR Solution PJ09-W2-44 describes operational environments where airspace structures and responsibilities adapt dynamically to traffic demand. In such environments, traditional assumptions based on fixed sector geometries become less applicable.

The FINEST cooperation illustrates the practical challenges of dynamic service provision, as the initiative seeks to make en route air traffic service provision independent of Flight Information Region (FIR) boundaries. This approach facilitates traffic management based on demand rather than on geographic constraints.

As a result, controller competence should not be defined exclusively by familiarity with a fixed geographic area. Rather, competence must encompass the ability to provide air traffic services safely and effectively under diverse and dynamic system conditions. This perspective is consistent with public annual reports on the Estonia-Finland cooperation, which emphasise harmonised working methods, common operational practices and the flexible deployment of controllers within an integrated service area.

This shift has substantial implications beginning at the earliest stages of the ATCO lifecycle. In dynamic, cross-border environments, early training should incorporate variability, delegation of responsibility and system-level thinking as standard operational characteristics. The FINEST cooperation indicates that harmonising procedures, training and competency schemes is essential for efficient cross-border service provision, suggesting that controllers must be prepared from the outset to operate within a shared operational framework rather than a solely national one.

SESAR interoperability work (PJ.10-W2-93) describes cross-border operational environments (such as FINEST) where service provision spans organisational boundaries. In such contexts, validation and endorsement approaches need to support flexible deployment and shared operational concepts.

Within the FINEST environment, cross-border service provision is not just an airspace delegation project between two ANSPs but a shift towards a shared operational system and working methods. Based on the SESAR interoperability concept (PJ.10-W2-93), enabling such operations requires more than technical connectivity; it also requires aligned procedures, common working methods and a consistent approach to managing traffic on both sides.

In practical terms, controllers are no longer operating strictly within national boundaries or fixed sectors. Instead, they are working within a combined operational framework in which responsibility areas can shift based on traffic demand and sector configuration. This means that the focus shifts from purely local knowledge to the ability to operate within a harmonised system, using agreed procedures and coordination methods.

In this setup, competence cannot be tied only to a specific sector or airspace block. Controllers need to be able to handle traffic across different configurations and under shared procedures, regardless of which side of the border the service is delivered from. The operational concept assumes that traffic should be managed seamlessly, without visible differences in service delivery.

This directly affects how validation and endorsement should be approached. Traditional models based on fixed sector validation become less suitable when sectors are dynamic and responsibilities can move between ANSPs. Instead, there is a need to ensure that competence is transferable and recognised across the whole operational area. Controllers must be validated against the common operational concept, not just against a local sector layout.

From a FINEST perspective, this makes competence portability a key enabler. If controllers cannot be deployed flexibly across the integrated environment, the benefits of cross-border operations – such as improved capacity, better workload distribution and increased resilience – cannot be fully realised. As a result, validation and endorsement processes need to evolve to reflect how the system actually operates, while still maintaining safety, regulatory compliance and transparency.

A key enabler of such cross-border operations in practice is the harmonisation of ATCO procedures to the maximum extent possible. Without a common way of working, differences in how traffic is managed, coordinated and resolved would quickly lead to inconsistencies, increased workload and reduced predictability. For the concept to function as intended, controllers on both sides must apply the same operational logic, use the same phraseology principles and follow aligned coordination practices.

This level of harmonisation is closely linked to the supporting technical environment. The use of a common or highly aligned ATM system significantly reduces complexity by ensuring that controllers interact with traffic information, tools and decision-support functions in the same way. Differences in system behaviour or interface design can otherwise introduce unnecessary cognitive load and increase the risk of misunderstandings in a shared operational environment.
