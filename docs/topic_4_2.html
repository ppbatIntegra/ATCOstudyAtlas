Emerging operational concepts

Final Report · §7.1 · Future operational and technical changes

The first step in our analysis was to examine how future ATM tools, operational concepts, and general technical evolution affect air traffic controllers’ work. To this end, we analysed seven of the key emerging operational concepts from the SESAR framework:

  • Trajectory-based Operations (TBO)
  • Advanced Flexible Use of Airspace (A-FUA) and Dynamic Airspace Configuration (DAC)
  • Complex CPDLC clearances
  • Remote Tower Operations (including digital towers)
  • Advanced conflict detection and automated resolution support
  • Automatic speech recognition and attention guidance
  • AI-based ATC digital assistants.

These seven concepts represent important elements of the SESAR Master Plan or emerging technical changes, including AI. They provide a starting point for examining how technology may change ATCO tasks.

Trajectory-based operations progressively replace a succession of locally managed tactical interventions with the collaborative management of shared, continuously updated four-dimensional flight trajectories. Airborne, ground and network systems exchange trajectory intentions, constraints and predictions to support a more consistent gate-to-gate picture. For ATCO tasks, this may shift effort away from repeatedly reconstructing the likely flight path and towards assessing proposed trajectory changes, managing deviations and intervening where predictions or constraints require it. Automated trajectory prediction, conformance monitoring and synchronisation are essential enablers.

Advanced Flexible Use of Airspace (A-FUA) and Dynamic Airspace Configuration (DAC) aim to make the allocation and use of airspace more responsive to civil and military needs. Real-time exchange of airspace status, variable-profile reservations and collaborative planning tools allow configurations to change closer to the time of operation and, ultimately, across borders. Rather than working predominantly with static airspace allocations, ATCOs may need to interpret continuously updated availability and configuration information, accommodate changes in traffic planning and coordinate through increasingly system-supported processes. Here, automation primarily supports information exchange, constraint management and dynamic coordination.

SESAR uses the term “lateral and vertical complex CPDLC clearances” for advanced data-link procedures capable of communicating multi-element trajectory changes and constraints, potentially further in advance than conventional tactical voice clearances. Ground automation, downlinked flight-management-system trajectory information, and inter-centre coordination help controllers evaluate and transmit changes that affect larger portions of a flight. This may replace some sequences of discrete verbal instructions with the review, authorisation and monitoring of a more integrated clearance and associated trajectory. The exact distribution of tasks remains dependent on operational procedures and the level of automation implemented.

Remote aerodrome air traffic services enable tower services to be delivered without direct visual observation of the aerodrome, using camera-based views, surveillance sources, communications and digital information overlays. Multiple remote tower concepts extend this arrangement to services for more than one aerodrome from a shared centre or module. ATCO tasks may consequently rely more heavily on digitally mediated observation, sensor fusion, automated alerts, prioritisation and switching between operating contexts. In these concepts, automation chiefly changes how the controller acquires and organises operational information; it should not be conflated with autonomous provision of aerodrome control.

Advanced separation-management tools combine improved trajectory prediction, conflict detection and possible resolution proposals. SESAR Solution #0468, for example, explores “what-next” proposals covering headings, speeds, levels and direct routings, supported by weather-aware conflict assessment. Part of the conflict-searching and option-generation workload could move to the system, with controllers interpreting predictions and comparing, selecting or modifying proposals.

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) converts controller–pilot voice exchanges into structured information. SESAR has explored their use to check issued instructions against system data, maintain command histories, update displays and reduce manual inputs. Complementary attention-guidance tools analyse traffic interactions and visually de-emphasise flights not expected to require immediate spacing action, while highlighting those more likely to need attention. Taken together, these concepts could reduce routine transcription, data entry and undifferentiated display scanning, while increasing the importance of checking machine-generated interpretations and attention cues.

SESAR’s ATC Digital Assistant concept explores the use of AI to support several distinct functions: tactical conflict-resolution advice, identification of flight-efficiency opportunities, short-term traffic forecasting and correction of flight plans. Unlike a tool dedicated to a single task, an assistant could support a wider sequence of analytical and preparatory activities. ATCO work could therefore evolve from performing each analysis separately towards evaluating and supervising a set of machine-generated assessments and proposals. This remains a research-oriented example; its potential effects on operational task allocation should not be presented as established deployment practice.

The SESAR automation roadmap

Final Report · §7.1 · Nature of the change in ATCO tasks

The concepts can also be read as a progression in the functions assigned to automation, from information handling towards wider operational action. The categories below are analytical rather than formal SESAR capability levels.

Nature of the change in ATCO tasks. Source: study elaboration based on SESAR sources.

Nature of changeIllustrative SESAR conceptsPotential change in ATCO tasks
Acquires, processes and presents informationRemote/digital towers; ASR; attention guidanceDirect visual observation, manual entry and broad scanning increasingly complemented by fused displays, machine interpretation and selective attention cues.
Predicts operational situationsTBO; advanced conflict detection; arrival-management toolsController prediction and anticipation increasingly informed by calculated trajectories, expected conflicts and forecast operational states.
Develops and evaluates solutionsAutomated conflict resolution; surface routing; integrated sequencingManual construction and comparison of options increasingly supported by generated and evaluated proposals.
Supports execution and coordinationComplex CPDLC clearances; advanced civil–military coordinationDiscrete voice and manual coordination steps increasingly supported by structured, digitally exchanged and system-checked workflows.
Potentially assumes broader operational activitiesAI-based ATC assistants; higher automation levelsMultiple analytical or routine functions may be carried out by automation, with the controller supervising, validating or intervening as appropriate to the level and operating scope.

Across these examples, we can observe an increased reliance on automation, which progressively supports or performs components of tasks historically carried out by controllers: information acquisition, situation prediction, solution generation, coordination and, at higher levels, action implementation. This suggests a systematic change in the balance between direct task execution and the review, supervision and intervention activities associated with automated systems.

This direction is explicit in the European ATM Master Plan (2025 edition). Its Automation Roadmap envisages ATM at automation level 2 by 2035 and, for certain flight phases and within a predefined operating scope, level 4 by 2045. These are strategic targets, not descriptions of today’s operational baseline. The roadmap’s levels distinguish decision support, resolution support, conditional automation and confined automation for examining task redistribution.

Understanding these changes requires analysis of individual tasks and how they may be redistributed between controllers and automation. The task taxonomy and human-role analysis provides that level of detail.
