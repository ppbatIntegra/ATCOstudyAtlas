ATCO licensing and CBTA

From unit-based validation to competency-based licensing · From unit-based validation to competency-based licensing

Today, ATCO validation is primarily unit- and position-based. Under Regulation (EU) 2015/340, an ATCO becomes operationally qualified for a particular sector, group of sectors or working position by completing an approved unit endorsement course over a defined number of hours. This includes local procedures and task-specific training, normally followed by on-the-job training in live traffic. The final assessment must take place in the operational environment under normal operational conditions at least once at the end of OJT. The detailed training programme, duration, assessment arrangements and performance objectives are largely defined locally in the Unit Training Plan and approved by the competent authority. This approach to unit endorsement bundles together skills, competences, knowledge elements, and behaviours expected of ATCOs, without explicitly defining what is required of them.

Competency-based training (CBTA) shifts the focus from merely completing a locally planned training schedule and accumulating X hours on specific tasks to validating actual competence. Regulation (EU) 2025/2143, which incorporates CBTA principles into ATCO training starting in 2029, mandates a clear connection between required performance and the competencies being developed and evaluated. Demonstrating competence involves multiple observations in various contexts, employing a tailored competency model and explicit performance standards. Achieving successful qualification requires demonstrating integrated performance across all relevant competencies.

A fully competency-based licensing model would take this logic one step further. Instead of treating qualification for an ATS unit as the principal evidence of competence, licensing could explicitly recognise three complementary domains: core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence, and operational-environment competence. An ATCO moving between units would then not automatically repeat a complete local qualification process. Existing competencies could be recognised, and training and assessment could focus on the competency gap—for example, a different ATM system, different airspace or procedures, or a more demanding traffic environment.

SES performance and national material

Intermediate Report · §1.1.1 and §1.1.5

To gain insight into capacity performance issues, planned measures and details on the ATCO situation and ATCO training, the performance plans and annual monitoring reports of the Single European Sky (SES) Member States were analysed from the third and fourth reference periods (RP3 and RP4).

As the practice was introduced in RP3, and data provision was on a voluntary basis (i.e. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2019/317 does not require Member States to provide this data), some Member States did not provide the complete dataset, limiting their submissions to, for example, the number of ATCOs in OPS at the end of each year. Further to this, data quality was also questionable during the first submissions, as Member States interpreted the calculation of ATCOs in OPS FTEs differently. Both of these limitations improved substantially during RP3, to the extent that the RP4 performance plans provide a reasonably complete dataset with an acceptable data quality.

In addition to the data tables and sources detailed above, we also conducted an initial desk research to collect publicly available national legislative acts and regulatory documents concerning ATCO training, licensing and rostering. While these documents typically remain at a high level, some do set out parameters that constrain the day-to-day operations of the affected ANSPs. These legal acts and regulatory documents are compiled into comprehensive tables, which will be completed during the ANSP interviews to the extent possible.

ATM/ANS providers and ATCO working conditions

The logical model of the ATCO management cycle · Regulations and legal constraints

EU regulations (such as Implementing Regulations (EU) 2017/373 and 2015/340, and various EU Directives) set out broad parameters for defining the working conditions of ATCOs and operational staff at ANSPs. These regulations ensure fair and equal treatment of the workforce across Europe and that safety requirements are duly considered and met. However, these regulations do not impose specific schemes or practical constraints on the management of ANSPs, as they are generic by design and leave ample room for interpretation by local authorities and policymakers.

All states have labour laws that align with European directives and define the general local rules of employment. Typically, these do not specify requirements or rules for aviation, or for air traffic management in particular, but remain at a more generic level. In some cases, national labour laws may specify rules on screen time, maximum shift lengths, mandatory rest periods, overtime conditions, etc.

While collective bargaining agreements (CBAs) between the employer and the labour unions are not regulations legally speaking, from a management perspective, they still introduce legal constraints into the decision-making, notwithstanding their importance in ensuring a level playing field and a fair treatment for employees. Even though most CBAs are not publicly available, it is still known, that there are huge differences between the topics included, the level of detail, the period of validity, and even the number of CBAs from ANSP to ANSP. The collective bargaining process and the accepted negotiation tactics are both culturally determined, and thus also vary greatly across Europe.

In addition to labour laws, governments may issue industry-specific legislation that includes high-level provisions on the working conditions of ATCOs (examples include Austria, Bulgaria, Poland, Romania, and Hungary, to name a few). In Belgium, France, and Spain, there are very detailed and specific local legislative acts governing the working conditions of ATCOs, even defining key parameters of the rostering schemes, and introducing various other constraints. Civil Aviation Authorities or National Supervisory Authorities may also issue local requirements or guidelines for ANSPs concerning ATCO working conditions, though this appears to be a less common practice.

RMT.0738

Final Report · Chapter 7, Next steps for competency-based licensing

While we are convinced that CBL offers a potential way to improve the training and licensing framework of ATCOs and to fulfil our objective of future-proofing, substantial work remains ahead concerning the definition of the details, procedures, requirements, etc.

We understand that the proposed CBL concept feeds into EASA’s rulemaking task 0738, and could be progressed through that process. However, we also recognise the need raise awareness about the concept and start educating the wider community and socialise the idea, so that experts involved ATCO training and licensing have ample time to consider it and provide their thoughts and contributions to the work lead by EASA. We also understand that even before the rulemaking task may start, DG MOVE will have to specify in more detail the objectives and expected outcomes of the process.
