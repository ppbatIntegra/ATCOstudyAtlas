Purpose and analytical perspective

Advanced SESAR operational concepts and the evolution of ATCO tasks · Purpose and analytical perspective

The examples below illustrate how selected SESAR developments may change the activities performed by air traffic controllers (ATCOs). The common theme is not simply the digitisation of existing work: automation increasingly acquires and processes information, anticipates future situations, develops operational options and supports their implementation. The examples vary substantially in maturity, and do not imply that all described functions are already deployed.

This direction is explicit in the European ATM Master Plan (2025 edition). Its Automation Roadmap envisages ATM at automation level 2 by 2035 and, for certain flight phases and within a predefined operating scope, level 4 by 2045. These are strategic targets, not descriptions of today’s operational baseline. The roadmap’s levels distinguish decision support, resolution support, conditional automation and confined automation, providing a useful analytical basis for examining task redistribution.

Operational concepts and tools

Advanced SESAR operational concepts and the evolution of ATCO tasks · Seven SESAR concepts

1 Trajectory-based operations (TBO)

Trajectory-based operations progressively replace a succession of locally managed tactical interventions with the collaborative management of shared, continuously updated four-dimensional flight trajectories. Airborne, ground and network systems exchange trajectory intentions, constraints and predictions to support a more consistent gate-to-gate picture. For ATCO tasks, this may shift effort away from repeatedly reconstructing the likely flight path and towards assessing proposed trajectory changes, managing deviations and intervening where predictions or constraints require it. Automated trajectory prediction, conformance monitoring and synchronisation are essential enablers.

2 Advanced civil–military coordination and dynamic airspace configuration

Advanced Flexible Use of Airspace (A-FUA) and Dynamic Airspace Configuration (DAC) aim to make the allocation and use of airspace more responsive to civil and military needs. Real-time exchange of airspace status, variable-profile reservations and collaborative planning tools allow configurations to change closer to the time of operation and, ultimately, across borders. Rather than working predominantly with static airspace allocations, ATCOs may need to interpret continuously updated availability and configuration information, accommodate changes in traffic planning and coordinate through increasingly system-supported processes. Here automation primarily supports information exchange, constraint management and dynamic coordination.

3 Complex lateral and vertical CPDLC clearances

SESAR uses the term “lateral and vertical complex CPDLC clearances” for advanced data-link procedures capable of communicating multi-element trajectory changes and constraints, potentially further in advance than conventional tactical voice clearances. Ground automation, downlinked flight-management-system trajectory information and inter-centre coordination help controllers evaluate and transmit changes affecting larger portions of a flight. This may replace some sequences of discrete verbal instructions with the review, authorisation and monitoring of a more integrated clearance and associated trajectory. The exact distribution of tasks remains dependent on operational procedures and the level of automation implemented.

4 Remote and digital tower operations

Remote aerodrome air traffic services enable tower services to be delivered without direct visual observation of the aerodrome, using camera-based views, surveillance sources, communications and digital information overlays. Multiple remote tower concepts extend this arrangement to services for more than one aerodrome from a shared centre or module. ATCO tasks may consequently rely more heavily on digitally mediated observation, sensor fusion, automated alerts, prioritisation and switching between operating contexts. In these concepts, automation chiefly changes how the controller acquires and organises operational information; it should not be conflated with autonomous provision of aerodrome control.

5 Advanced conflict detection and automated resolution support

Advanced separation-management tools combine improved trajectory prediction with automated detection of potential conflicts and generation of possible resolutions. SESAR Solution #0468, for example, explores “what-next” resolution proposals covering headings, speeds, levels and direct routings, supported by weather-aware conflict assessment. This can transfer part of the conflict-searching and option-generation workload from the controller to the system. The controller’s work correspondingly shifts towards interpreting predictions, comparing proposals, selecting or modifying a solution and monitoring its safe execution.

6 Automatic speech recognition and automated attention guidance

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) converts controller–pilot voice exchanges into structured information. SESAR has explored their use to check issued instructions against system data, maintain command histories, update displays and reduce manual inputs. Complementary attention-guidance tools analyse traffic interactions and visually de-emphasise flights not expected to require immediate spacing action, while highlighting those more likely to need attention. Taken together, these concepts could reduce routine transcription, data entry and undifferentiated display scanning, while increasing the importance of checking machine-generated interpretations and attention cues.

7 AI-based ATC digital assistants

SESAR’s ATC Digital Assistant concept explores the use of AI to support several distinct functions: tactical conflict-resolution advice, identification of flight-efficiency opportunities, short-term traffic forecasting and correction of flight plans. Unlike a tool dedicated to a single task, an assistant could support a wider sequence of analytical and preparatory activities. ATCO work could therefore evolve from performing each analysis separately towards evaluating and supervising a set of machine-generated assessments and proposals. This remains a research-oriented example; its potential effects on operational task allocation should not be presented as established deployment practice.

Nature of the change in ATCO tasks

The concepts can also be read as a progression in the functions assigned to automation, from information handling towards wider operational action. The categories below are analytical rather than formal SESAR capability levels.

Scope and limitations

Advanced SESAR operational concepts and the evolution of ATCO tasks · Synthesis and source note

Across these examples, increased reliance on automation does not mean an immediate move to autonomous ATC. Rather, automation progressively supports or performs components of tasks historically carried out by controllers: information acquisition, situation prediction, solution generation, coordination and, at higher levels, action implementation. This suggests a systematic change in the balance between direct task execution and the review, supervision and intervention activities associated with automated systems. The nature and scale of that change remain concept-specific and maturity-dependent.

Descriptions are based on public SESAR solution summaries, EASA guidance and the European ATM Master Plan. They are deliberately framed as potential task changes, not uniform statements of operational deployment or ATCO competency requirements.
