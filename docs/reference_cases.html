Retrospective case selection

Intermediate Report · Chapter 3, Identifying the ANSPs with ATCO shortages

In order to identify which ANSPs are struggling with ATCO shortages, we used en route ATFM delays registered under delay codes “C – ATC capacity” and “S – ATC staffing”. We calculated the share of total delay minutes under these codes relative to the ANSP’s all-cause total delay minutes (this is called the share of C+S delays), and also the contribution to the all-cause total delay minutes generated in the SES area (this is called the weight of C+S delays) for each ANSP and each year in the period. We must note that C+S delays do not include delays caused by industrial action by ATCOs and other ANSP personnel.

We then used the average values of the weight of C+S weights over the period for each ANSP to identify which ANSPs’ C+S delays had the highest weights on average during the period. We selected ANSPs with the five highest average C+S weights for deeper analysis. These ANSPs consistently registered relatively high shares of C+S delays, which also significantly contributed to the total delays experienced in the network. The selected ANSPs are:

  • DSNA
  • DFS
  • ENAIRE
  • HASP
  • HungaroControl.

The PRB conducted interviews with all selected ANSPs at the end of 2023 and the beginning of 2024 as part of the target-setting exercise for RP4. The summaries of these interviews were published as an annex to the PRB RP4 target report. The following sections summarise the information from the PRB report, as well as the outcomes of the ANSP interviews conducted in this study.

DSNA

Intermediate Report · Chapter 3, DSNA

During the PRB interviews, France and DSNA reported several factors that led to the apparent capacity performance issues. Firstly, the French government required all national government organisations to reduce their costs and cut personnel headcounts between 2010 and 2020. This requirement also applied to DSNA, leading to a decrease in the number of ATCOs. This was reportedly further exacerbated by the length of the training process, normal ATCO attrition and the impact of COVID-19 on the training. However, DSNA was optimistic at the time of the PRB interview that it had reached a turning point in training and could deliver a steady increase in the number of controllers over the RP4 years, despite a large retirement wave affecting around one third of the ATCO population between 2028 and 2035.

DSNA and France also highlighted the limitations in DSNA’s rostering scheme, which significantly constrain the allocation of ATCO resources and require higher staffing levels than would be needed with more flexible arrangements. As we noted in the previous section, France is one of the Member States in which the rostering scheme is specified in national legislation.

DFS

Intermediate Report · Chapter 3, DFS

Germany and DFS emphasised during the PRB interview that most of the capacity issues leading to high delays stemmed from structural changes and poor management decisions made in the past. DFS recognised the shortage of controllers in 2018, after delays related to the lack of ATCOs skyrocketed. While training efforts began in 2019 to rectify the situation, government-imposed restrictions during 2020 and 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic caused a nearly three-year delay in the training process, leaving around 120 controllers missing from operations. This shortage of ATCOs, combined with the concentrated and strong growth of traffic in the already congested Southern part of the airspace of Karlsruhe UAC and the impact from the increased military operations due to Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine, resulted in the record-high delays experienced in 2022.

On the other hand, DFS also reported that while traffic growth was strong in some parts of the upper airspace, traffic in the lower airspace seemed to be recovering more slowly, meaning that the lower airspace ACCs did not encounter staff shortages.

Figure 33 shows that the number of ATCOs (especially ACC ATCOs) began to decrease after 2016 and continued to decline until 2022, when it reached the lowest level since 2004. The delay figures shown in Figure 32 indicate that DFS may have found a solution to address the shortage of ATCOs; however, this trend is not yet visible in the ATCO figures. (To be confirmed through the interviews.)

ENAIRE

Intermediate Report · Chapter 3, ENAIRE

During the RP4 preparations, ENAIRE and Spain reported to the PRB that the delays experienced after the COVID-19 pandemic were due to a faster-than-expected traffic recovery, a structural shift in traffic patterns pushing flights into the Eastern and Southern parts of Spain, most notably to Sevilla ACC, which was combined with relatively low ATCO recruitment and training plans. However, once ENAIRE realised the growth in traffic, training and recruitment of additional ATCOs were immediately started, and ENAIRE planned to resolve the ATCO shortage by the first years of RP4. ENAIRE also mentioned limitations on the flexibility of the rostering scheme imposed by national legislation and the collective bargaining agreement; however, at the time of the interview, management was negotiating a potential update to the CBA to introduce stand-by shifts. The values in Figure 34 (previous page) suggest that some staff-related issues may have begun earlier in Spain, as C+S delays were already relatively high in 2018 and 2019. C+S delays exploded again in 2024 and 2025, largely driven by Sevilla ACC, suggesting that some issues remain unresolved. (To be confirmed during the interviews.)

The evolution of ACC ATCOs in OPS, as shown in Figure 35, indicates a steady decrease in the number of ATCOs between 2013 and 2018, which may have contributed to the shortages observed in 2018 and 2019. There is a minor increase in 2019 and 2020, but the number of ACC ATCOs appears to remain stable in the following years.

HASP

Intermediate Report · Chapter 3, HASP

The interview with Greece and HASP in the RP4 target-setting process revealed that the massive shortage experienced by HASP (around 100 ATCOs were missing from the ACCs, representing 20-40% of the ideal situation) was due to restrictions on HASP’s recruitment process imposed by national legislation. ATCOs are recruited through a lengthy public recruitment process, while the recruitment and training plans require annual approval from the government. Frequent changes in government policy decisions, delays in the training and recruitment process and a relatively swift recovery of traffic after the COVID-19 impact meant that HASP suffered from a shortage of ATCOs from 2023 onwards. The picture in Figure 36 shows that if HASP was able to resolve the ATCO shortage, it could accommodate the traffic with an acceptable level of delay.

While this was not discussed at the time, Figure 37 indicates that incoming ATCOs were directed to Greek airport units in 2021, where a significant increase in ATCO numbers is evident, whereas the number of ACC ATCOs remains flat. This practice may further exaggerate the shortage of ACC ATCOs in future years.

HungaroControl

Intermediate Report · Chapter 3, HungaroControl

During the PRB interview, HungaroControl reported that key limitations behind the C+S delays were the inflexibility of the rostering scheme, unexpected growth in traffic due to Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the training process and subsequent success rates. HungaroControl’s rostering scheme has limitations regarding the hours in position for ATCOs and the fixed length of shifts. Despite the options to introduce extra shifts during the summer period, due to the cumulative fatigue of ATCOs, the rostering scheme still does not allow for coping with the high seasonality of traffic.

HungaroControl reported that, although ATCO training was not stopped during the COVID-19 pandemic, social distancing requirements necessitated suspending the training process. This resulted in attrition during training and lower success rates at the end of training. In addition, HungaroControl reported increased ATCO attrition, partly due to retirements and partly to ATCOs leaving for other European ANSPs.

Finally, the impact of Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine led to highly concentrated traffic growth in the Eastern part of Hungarian airspace. Together with the missing controllers and the limitations of the rostering scheme, this led to the record-high delay in 2024, as shown in Figure 38.

The trend in the number of ATCOs in OPS shows an overall increase between 2011 and 2018, which was followed by a drop in 2019. This coincides with the delay figures shown above. Figures from 2020 onwards show steady growth; however, when viewed alongside the delay figures and the evolution of traffic, it appears that this growth was insufficient.

Summary

Intermediate Report · Chapter 3, Summary

The key finding – so far – from the analysis is that there is no dominant reason behind ATCO shortages in Europe: The top five contributors to ATCO shortage-related delays all reported different reasons, and also display different data. However, there are a few emerging commonalities.

Firstly, three ANSPs noted limitations in rostering schemes as a key reason for staff shortages. In France and Spain, this was partly due to the rigidity of national legislation specifying rostering parameters. In contrast, in Hungary, rostering is set by the ANSP (but is also subject to collective bargaining agreements).

Secondly, unexpected traffic shifts and growth, and the uneven distribution of traffic, were also mentioned as key reasons. It is yet unclear whether this indicates problems with planning and forecasting or the growing uncertainty in air transport.

All ANSPs mentioned the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and how restrictions and social distancing requirements were detrimental to ATCO training. However, in some cases, the ATCO numbers we observe in the datasets do not clearly confirm these issues.

Finally, an important emerging finding is that where the central administration of the state and/or the government imposes restrictions on the operation of the ANSP (either in the form of specifying operational procedures and parameters in national legislation or requiring government/ministerial approval for operational decisions), ATCO shortages and capacity problems appear to be more likely to occur. This may be due to the reduced ability to react to changes in the operational environment.

As for the study’s focus topic, none of the interviews indicated that European regulations constrained the training and licensing processes or served as a driver or reason for the issues.

FINEST

Intermediate Report · §6.1, FINEST

Within Europe, SESAR concepts are moving from theoretical frameworks to practical implementation. Cross-border service provision initiatives are beginning to incorporate elements of dynamic airspace and flexible service responsibility. A notable example is the bilateral cooperation between Estonia and Finland, referred to as the FINEST project. Publicly available sources indicate that this initiative facilitates dynamic cross-border air traffic service provision between the two Area Control Centres by employing harmonised procedures, common rostering principles and integrated technical systems.

The move towards dynamic, integrated service provision exposes fundamental limitations in traditional Air Traffic Controller (ATCO) training and lifecycle models. Historically, training, validation and licensing have relied on stable sector geometries, predictable traffic patterns and clearly defined areas of responsibility. SESAR Solution PJ09-W2-44 describes operational environments where airspace structures and responsibilities adapt dynamically to traffic demand. In such environments, traditional assumptions based on fixed sector geometries become less applicable.

The FINEST cooperation illustrates the practical challenges of dynamic service provision, as the initiative seeks to make en route air traffic service provision independent of Flight Information Region (FIR) boundaries. This approach facilitates traffic management based on demand rather than on geographic constraints.

As a result, controller competence should not be defined exclusively by familiarity with a fixed geographic area. Rather, competence must encompass the ability to provide air traffic services safely and effectively under diverse and dynamic system conditions. This perspective is consistent with public annual reports on the Estonia-Finland cooperation, which emphasise harmonised working methods, common operational practices and the flexible deployment of controllers within an integrated service area.

This shift has substantial implications beginning at the earliest stages of the ATCO lifecycle. In dynamic, cross-border environments, early training should incorporate variability, delegation of responsibility and system-level thinking as standard operational characteristics. The FINEST cooperation indicates that harmonising procedures, training and competency schemes is essential for efficient cross-border service provision, suggesting that controllers must be prepared from the outset to operate within a shared operational framework rather than a solely national one.

Within the FINEST environment, cross-border service provision is not just an airspace delegation project between two ANSPs but a shift towards a shared operational system and working methods. Based on the SESAR interoperability concept (PJ.10-W2-93), enabling such operations requires more than technical connectivity; it also requires aligned procedures, common working methods and a consistent approach to managing traffic on both sides.

From a FINEST perspective, this makes competence portability a key enabler. If controllers cannot be deployed flexibly across the integrated environment, the benefits of cross-border operations – such as improved capacity, better workload distribution and increased resilience – cannot be fully realised. As a result, validation and endorsement processes need to evolve to reflect how the system actually operates, while still maintaining safety, regulatory compliance and transparency.

A key enabler of such cross-border operations in practice is the harmonisation of ATCO procedures to the maximum extent possible. Without a common way of working, differences in how traffic is managed, coordinated and resolved would quickly lead to inconsistencies, increased workload and reduced predictability. For the concept to function as intended, controllers on both sides must apply the same operational logic, use the same phraseology principles and follow aligned coordination practices.

This level of harmonisation is closely linked to the supporting technical environment. The use of a common or highly aligned ATM system significantly reduces complexity by ensuring that controllers interact with traffic information, tools and decision-support functions in the same way. Differences in system behaviour or interface design can otherwise introduce unnecessary cognitive load and increase the risk of misunderstandings in a shared operational environment.
