Benefits and next steps

Final Report · Chapter 7, Why competency-based licensing is future-proof?

As we will see in this section, CBL has many practical advantages across different time horizons; however, that does not mean it fulfils the objective of being future-proof. We propose this concept to future-proof the framework because it offers flexibility at various levels while maintaining a stable, clear structure.

On the one hand, the CBL concept requires specifying competencies in all three domains and defining the appropriate performance standards and observable behaviours. However, competent authorities and NSAs can set the standards and observable behaviours for the specific ATS units, and update these as necessary in line with the evolution of the technical and operational environment. This also applies at the competency level: even if the list of competencies needs revision or updating, the overall structure and licensing logic remain the same, creating much-needed continuity and stability in the system.

Second, the CBL approach fits naturally with competency-based training, improving consistency between the training and licensing parts of the future framework, another aspect of future-proofing.

Finally, a more modular approach to licensing and unit endorsement supports advanced operational concepts and improved cooperation between ATS units, especially cross-border initiatives, as well as ATCO mobility between units (more in this in the following paragraphs). This means that CBL can not only be adapted to changing competency requirements, but can also accommodate new service delivery models and more dynamic arrangements.

To fully illustrate the potential of the CBL logic, we divided the foreseen practical benefits into three groups: conceptual, licensing, and framework benefits, as illustrated in Figure 18. Conceptual benefits arise from applying the logic within the current set of rules, using the flexibility provided by the current framework (described in earlier chapters of the study). These benefits can be realised almost immediately. Licensing benefits become available as targeted regulatory work is carried out, CBL is implemented progressively, and licensing practices are transformed. Based on the current plans (with EASA’s RMT.0738 officially starting in 2029), these benefits are expected no sooner than a three-to-six-year horizon. Finally, we understand that framework benefits will be enabled when the system fully transitions to CBL logic, extending the concept's implementation into further areas, which is not expected before the end of this decade, and possibly even later than that. While this timeline may seem long to realise the full potential, it lets the community first implement CBTA, then complete the required targeted regulatory work, and gradually close the ATCO gap at the same time.

If and when the regulatory work is complete, and CBL is fully implemented as foreseen, further benefits become available. By moving ATCO training and validation logic to a competency-based approach, individualised training pathways become more accessible and easier to monitor and oversee. If the CBL approach delivers the foreseen licensing benefits, some procedural approvals for ATCO training could be removed. This would also boost innovation in the training domain, which would already be enabled by the move towards the competency-based logic in training: when the validation focuses on what competences the ATCO needs to demonstrate and against which standards, with what kind of observable behaviour, the tools, methodologies, systems, and courses used during the training matter less from an oversight and approval perspective, without compromising safety and the credibility of oversight functions. In fact, a harmonised set of competences across all three domains, with explicitly defined, locally specific required standards and observable behaviours for each competency, could strengthen oversight credibility and further improve overall safety.

Beyond direct application to ATCO training and licensing, the CBL concept could also be rolled out for instructors, assessors, and potentially other roles. On the one hand, instructors require a specific set of pedagogical competencies, and assessors also require specific competencies to perform their tasks. These could serve as the basis for instructor and assessor endorsements. On the other hand, if we consider how training may be transformed by the three-domain approach, separate instructor roles may be defined for the specific domains (at least to a certain extent). Depending on whether and how this becomes possible, different requirements for the specific instructor roles may help alleviate the shortage of operational instructors, which ANSPs reported as a major constraint during stakeholder discussions.

Next steps for competency-based licensing

While we are convinced that CBL offers a potential way to improve the training and licensing framework of ATCOs and to fulfil our objective of future-proofing, substantial work remains ahead concerning the definition of the details, procedures, requirements, etc.

We understand that the proposed CBL concept feeds into EASA’s rulemaking task 0738, and could be progressed through that process. However, we also recognise the need raise awareness about the concept and start educating the wider community and socialise the idea, so that experts involved ATCO training and licensing have ample time to consider it and provide their thoughts and contributions to the work lead by EASA. We also understand that even before the rulemaking task may start, DG MOVE will have to specify in more detail the objectives and expected outcomes of the process.

Limits of the indicative analysis

Final Report · §7.2, Generic task taxonomy and its analysis

We performed the analysis to explore the future effects of increasing automation; therefore, the results are indicative only and should be tested against the relevant operational concepts and safety cases before any further use outside this study.
