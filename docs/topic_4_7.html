Training and assessment consequences

The three-domain structure provides a common language for training objectives and assessment. Automation also changes the capabilities that need deliberate practice and maintenance.

Competency-based training and assessment

Final Report · §7.6 · Conceptual benefits

Defining requirements across all three competence domains can help trace the capabilities needed for an ATS unit back to the aptitudes, knowledge, skills and attitudes developed through selection and training. The six-layer competence model explains these relationships.

The three domains provide a shared structure for defining training requirements, objectives and phases. This is particularly relevant where training is partly outsourced or liberalised, and when defining transitional, pre-OJT and OJT content.

Finally, we also consider the notion that the concept is a natural extension of the CBTA methodology, offering a conceptual benefit by making socialising the concept easier and implementation smoother (if and when that happens).

Consequences of automation for competencies

Final Report · §7.2 · Competency evolution

The task analysis also explores how core competencies may evolve if each task’s full automation potential is realised.

The foreseen evolution of competency under automation. Source: study elaboration.

Competency or requirementExpected evolution
Situational awarenessRemains fundamental but expands into system, mode, data-quality, uncertainty and automation awareness.
Traffic and capacity managementMoves from tactical traffic manipulation toward constraint-setting, supervisory flow management and network optimisation.
Separation and conflict resolutionMoves from routine execution toward assurance, approval, intervention and recovery, while retaining fallback understanding.
CommunicationLess routine phraseology; more ambiguity resolution, urgent communication, exception handling and cross-system negotiation.
CoordinationLess routine transaction processing; more exception negotiation, shared-intent management and responsibility-boundary awareness.
Management of non-routine situationsBecomes substantially more important as the human role concentrates on novelty, emergencies, disruption and degraded operations.
Problem-solving and decision-makingBecomes less frequent in routine operations but more difficult, uncertain and consequential at the human–automation authority boundary.
Self-managementExpands to vigilance, trust calibration, startle management, readiness, accountability and maintaining competence despite limited practice.
Workload managementShifts from sustained traffic workload to underload, attention management, alert discipline, mode transitions and sudden workload peaks.
TeamworkExpands into human–automation teaming, shared mode awareness and coordination across interconnected automated organisations.
Automation and system management (additional competency added by the study)Understand, monitor and manage automated functions, their modes, authority, data dependencies, uncertainty, interactions, limitations and failure behaviour.
Skill maintenance (additional enabler added by the study)Ensure that rarely exercised intervention, manual fallback and recovery competencies remain available through recurrent simulation, deliberate practice, recency rules and realistic degraded-mode assessment.

Based on the evolution of tasks and changes in the human role, we provisionally added a competency for automation and system management, and an important enabler for skill maintenance to ensure that competencies rarely used in an automated environment remain available.

Instructors and assessors

Final Report · §7.6 · Framework benefits

Beyond direct application to ATCO training and licensing, the CBL concept could also be rolled out for instructors, assessors, and potentially other roles. On the one hand, instructors require a specific set of pedagogical competencies, and assessors also require specific competencies to perform their tasks. These could serve as the basis for instructor and assessor endorsements. On the other hand, if we consider how training may be transformed by the three-domain approach, separate instructor roles may be defined for the specific domains (at least to a certain extent). Depending on whether and how this becomes possible, different requirements for the specific instructor roles may help alleviate the shortage of operational instructors, which ANSPs reported as a major constraint during stakeholder discussions.
