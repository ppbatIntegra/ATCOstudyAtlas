Final Report

Final Report · Objectives and future-framework summary

The primary objective of the study is to assess whether the current European ATCO licensing framework and workforce management practices can support future ATM operational, technological, and workforce requirements, and to identify potential improvements that could strengthen the long-term availability of qualified controllers across the European ATM network.

To achieve this objective, the study pursues four complementary goals:

  • To establish a common understanding of the current European ATCO workforce situation and the factors influencing controller availability;
  • To assess the regulatory, organisational, operational and training-related challenges affecting the ATCO management cycle;
  • To identify existing best practices and potential future practices capable of addressing those challenges; and
  • To develop evidence-based recommendations supporting the future evolution of the European ATCO licensing framework and workforce management practices.

The study covers the Member States of EASA and adopts a projection horizon extending to 2040. The assessment examines the full ATCO management cycle, including workforce planning, recruitment, selection, training, licensing, operational deployment and retention.

The study focuses on workforce availability and the factors influencing the relationship between future demand and supply of controllers. Economic considerations, including cost-benefit assessments of individual measures, fall outside the scope of the analysis.

A secondary objective of the study is to examine how the ATCO training and licensing scheme could be prepared for future changes in technology and new service delivery models, and to propose a possible way forward for future-proofing the framework.

Automation, new ATM systems, cross-border operations, and evolving service-delivery models increasingly change what controllers do and how responsibilities are shared between humans and systems. The current training and licensing framework relies on ratings and unit endorsements that bundle together a wide range of capabilities. This limits transparency on which competencies are transferable and can make mobility and requalification more burdensome than necessary.

The study addresses this by developing a task- and competency-based analytical framework for the ATCO role. It examines how automation changes individual ATCO tasks, separates aptitude, knowledge, skills and attitudes, competence, demonstrated performance and formal qualification, and identifies which elements of competence are generic and which depend on a specific technical or operational environment. A key contribution of the study is the resulting three-domain model of core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence and operational-environment competence, providing a structured basis for analysing transferability, training needs and qualification gaps.

Building on this analysis, the study develops a competency-based licensing (CBL) concept in which qualification focuses more directly on what an ATCO can demonstrably do, in which technical environment and under which operational conditions. Rather than treating a change of unit or environment as requiring a largely complete requalification pathway, the concept enables the recognition of already demonstrated competencies and focuses training and assessment on the remaining gaps. This provides a basis for more modular, targeted training; greater mobility and cross-border deployment; better recognition of prior competence; and a licensing framework that can evolve with technology and automation while maintaining the required level of operational safety.

Intermediate Report

Intermediate Report · Historical findings and management cycle

The historical assessment combines more than 20 years of workforce, utilisation, productivity, traffic and delay data. Four findings summarise the analysis.

  • The evolution of the number of ATCOs in OPS clearly did not keep pace with increasing traffic demand; furthermore, most ANSPs showed significant year-on-year variations in ATCO numbers. This indicates a lack of a long-term ATCO staffing plan and/or difficulties in executing such plans. We understand that ANSPs need to commit to a steady, long-term increase in ATCO numbers, as the traffic demand is forecast to grow continuously.
  • The utilisation of ATCO resources declined during the period: Most ANSPs recorded fewer hours on duty per controller in 2023 than in 2002. While this may have increased operational sustainability, it still negatively affects capacity output. Ideally, ATCO utilisation should be set at a level that is sustainable in the long term (while also accounting for fatigue management) and kept stable over the coming years.
  • On the positive side, most ANSPs managed to improve ATCO productivity, an important component of capacity output. ATCO productivity must remain in the focus of ANSPs, as it can also be a cost-efficient way of improving capacity performance.
  • It is clear from the datasets that the change in capacity output resulting from the evolution of ATCO numbers, ATCO utilisation and ATCO productivity was not sufficient to accommodate the growth in traffic. While there are significant local differences and positive examples, the overall picture on the network level remains alarming.

The ATCO pipeline describes the steps and decisions through which ANSPs plan, develop and manage controller resources.

The pipeline focuses on management processes, linking workforce planning to recruitment, training, qualification, operational deployment and feedback.

The pipeline provides a common framework for comparing ANSP practices, considering regulatory constraints and identifying where decisions at one stage affect later outcomes.
