Competency-based licensing

Final Report · Chapter 7, Our proposed solution for future-proofing

Combining everything we learned from the analyses, we developed the concept of Competency-Based Licensing (CBL), built on the three competence domains, and defined it as follows.

Competency-based licensing is an approach in which the scope of an ATCO’s licence privileges is explicitly linked to demonstrated competence across three complementary domains: core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence and operational-environment competence.

The core competence domain defines what an ATCO should be able to do: manage traffic and separation, communicate and coordinate, make decisions, and apply teamwork, self-management and workload management in routine and non-routine situations (building on the core competencies defined under CBTA).

The technical-system competence domain defines which systems and how the ATCO should be able to use: how to operate systems and tools, interpret outputs and understand limitations, supervise automation and respond to degraded or inappropriate system behaviour. This also brings in the perspective of evolving ATM systems and tools, increasing automation levels, and new technologies. These developments can and must be translated into competencies.

The operational-environment competence domain defines the operational environment in which the ATCO needs to operate and how: applying knowledge of the airspace, traffic, and procedures, including local arrangements; anticipating interactions; and adapting to changing configurations and operational complexity. This domain ensures that the local perspective is well defined and is translated into competencies.

Each competence domain comprises competencies built from knowledge, skills, and attitudes, which are explicitly defined and recognised separately, rather than relying on a single unit endorsement as the evidence for the entire bundle. The approach allows interaction between the competence domains but requires that all three domains are maintained, and that none can be completely replaced by competence in the other two domains. This notion is also widely supported by the interviews and stakeholder discussions.

The CBL concept builds on the foundation laid down by the CBTA regulation and draws on system-based licensing, with the key distinction that the operational-environment competence domain must be retained, even if ATCOs can use advanced system functionality. As we will explain in the following sections, this concept has many advantages compared to the purely system-based approach.

The existing architecture of ATCO licensing can be described as the combination of the rating, the rating endorsements where applicable, and the unit endorsement (and potentially additional endorsements). A conceptual future licence architecture based on CBL could consist of the core competence qualification, the technical-system qualification or endorsement, and the operational-environment qualification or endorsement. This would mean a shift in licensing towards relying on explicitly demonstrated competency components rather than primarily using the named ATS unit as the proxy for competence.

Current validation and CBTA

From unit-based validation to competency-based licensing · From unit-based validation to competency-based licensing

Today, ATCO validation is primarily unit- and position-based. Under Regulation (EU) 2015/340, an ATCO becomes operationally qualified for a particular sector, group of sectors or working position by completing an approved unit endorsement course over a defined number of hours. This includes local procedures and task-specific training, normally followed by on-the-job training in live traffic. The final assessment must take place in the operational environment under normal operational conditions at least once at the end of OJT. The detailed training programme, duration, assessment arrangements and performance objectives are largely defined locally in the Unit Training Plan and approved by the competent authority. This approach to unit endorsement bundles together skills, competences, knowledge elements, and behaviours expected of ATCOs, without explicitly defining what is required of them.

Competency-based training (CBTA) shifts the focus from merely completing a locally planned training schedule and accumulating X hours on specific tasks to validating actual competence. Regulation (EU) 2025/2143, which incorporates CBTA principles into ATCO training starting in 2029, mandates a clear connection between required performance and the competencies being developed and evaluated. Demonstrating competence involves multiple observations in various contexts, employing a tailored competency model and explicit performance standards. Achieving successful qualification requires demonstrating integrated performance across all relevant competencies.

A fully competency-based licensing model would take this logic one step further. Instead of treating qualification for an ATS unit as the principal evidence of competence, licensing could explicitly recognise three complementary domains: core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence, and operational-environment competence. An ATCO moving between units would then not automatically repeat a complete local qualification process. Existing competencies could be recognised, and training and assessment could focus on the competency gap—for example, a different ATM system, different airspace or procedures, or a more demanding traffic environment.

Benefits and next steps

Final Report · Chapter 7, Conceptual, licensing and framework benefits

As we will see in this section, CBL has many practical advantages across different time horizons; however, that does not mean it fulfils the objective of being future-proof. We propose this concept to future-proof the framework because it offers flexibility at various levels while maintaining a stable, clear structure.

On the one hand, the CBL concept requires specifying competencies in all three domains and defining the appropriate performance standards and observable behaviours. However, competent authorities and NSAs can set the standards and observable behaviours for the specific ATS units, and update these as necessary in line with the evolution of the technical and operational environment. This also applies at the competency level: even if the list of competencies needs revision or updating, the overall structure and licensing logic remain the same, creating much-needed continuity and stability in the system.

Second, the CBL approach fits naturally with competency-based training, improving consistency between the training and licensing parts of the future framework, another aspect of future-proofing.

Finally, a more modular approach to licensing and unit endorsement supports advanced operational concepts and improved cooperation between ATS units, especially cross-border initiatives, as well as ATCO mobility between units (more in this in the following paragraphs). This means that CBL can not only be adapted to changing competency requirements, but can also accommodate new service delivery models and more dynamic arrangements.

Conceptual benefits

Within the current framework, the logic of using the three competence domains already offers a solid structure to describe the competency requirements for ATCOs: if competency requirements are defined for an ATS unit in all three domains, these can be traced back along the model described in Figure 17 all the way to the underlying aptitudes and attributes required from candidates, and the knowledge, skill, and attitude elements that need to be transferred during the training.

The CBL concept offers further benefits for controller training, as it provides an improved and harmonised structure for discussing training requirements, objectives and defining training phases. This is particularly important for situations where ATCO training is partially outsourced and/or liberalised, as it provides a transparent and universal framework for the discussions. CBL and the three competence domains may also become useful when ANSPs and training organisations are defining the contents and objectives of the transitional, pre-OJT and OJT training stages (this area was discussed and indicated as somewhat problematic by several stakeholders).

Finally, we also consider the notion that the concept is a natural extension of CBTA logic, offering a conceptual benefit by making socialising the concept easier and implementation smoother (if and when that happens).

Licensing benefits

Arguably, the biggest licensing benefit of the CBL concept comes from its modular approach, which turns binary unit endorsement into a more nuanced and flexible exercise. This is achieved first by moving the focus from the ATS unit to the competences required by ATS units in the three domains, making unit endorsements comparable. When CBL is implemented, even if unit endorsements are retained, endorsements for specific units would rely on the qualifications in each competence domain, meaning the performance standards and observable behaviours per competency required for each unit would provide a structured, transparent means of comparison.

Not only would this allow for comparison of different unit requirements, but it would also allow comparison of an ATCO’s licence privileges with the requirements of a given unit. This would open up the possibility of recognising and accepting the qualifications an ATCO has achieved that meet the unit requirements, and only requiring training and a qualification for the gap. We understand this approach would be significantly more flexible while also being more structured and harmonised than current practice.

As a specific use-case, we also highlight that more dynamic cross-border or cross-unit airspace delegation solutions may also benefit significantly from this approach, especially if the technical-system domain remains largely stable during the delegation (meaning that ATCOs receiving the delegation use their “own” ATM system environment to provide the service). In such cases, cross-training and then endorsement could focus on the missing qualifications in the operational-environment domain (assuming the same requirements in the core competence domain, of course).

Finally, shifting oversight and approval toward competence requirements and their assessment, rather than training courses and materials, may also reduce administrative burden.

Framework benefits

If and when the regulatory work is complete, and CBL is fully implemented as foreseen, further benefits become available. By moving ATCO training and validation logic to a competency-based approach, individualised training pathways become more accessible and easier to monitor and oversee. If the CBL approach delivers the foreseen licensing benefits, some procedural approvals for ATCO training could be removed. This would also boost innovation in the training domain, which would already be enabled by the move towards the competency-based logic in training: when the validation focuses on what competences the ATCO needs to demonstrate and against which standards, with what kind of observable behaviour, the tools, methodologies, systems, and courses used during the training matter less from an oversight and approval perspective, without compromising safety and the credibility of oversight functions. In fact, a harmonised set of competences across all three domains, with explicitly defined, locally specific required standards and observable behaviours for each competency, could strengthen oversight credibility and further improve overall safety.

Beyond direct application to ATCO training and licensing, the CBL concept could also be rolled out for instructors, assessors, and potentially other roles. On the one hand, instructors require a specific set of pedagogical competencies, and assessors also require specific competencies to perform their tasks. These could serve as the basis for instructor and assessor endorsements. On the other hand, if we consider how training may be transformed by the three-domain approach, separate instructor roles may be defined for the specific domains (at least to a certain extent). Depending on whether and how this becomes possible, different requirements for the specific instructor roles may help alleviate the shortage of operational instructors, which ANSPs reported as a major constraint during stakeholder discussions.

Next steps for competency-based licensing

While we are convinced that CBL offers a potential way to improve the training and licensing framework of ATCOs and to fulfil our objective of future-proofing, substantial work remains ahead concerning the definition of the details, procedures, requirements, etc.

We understand that the proposed CBL concept feeds into EASA’s rulemaking task 0738, and could be progressed through that process. However, we also recognise the need raise awareness about the concept and start educating the wider community and socialise the idea, so that experts involved ATCO training and licensing have ample time to consider it and provide their thoughts and contributions to the work lead by EASA. We also understand that even before the rulemaking task may start, DG MOVE will have to specify in more detail the objectives and expected outcomes of the process.
