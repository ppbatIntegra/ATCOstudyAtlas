1 · Unit training

Final Report · §4.3, Unit Training

Unit Training comprises the training activities required before a trainee can operate independently within a specific operational environment. It includes transitional training, simulator activities, on-the-job training (OJT), validation processes and the associated instructor and assessor resources. As the final stage of the ATCO production pipeline before operational deployment, Unit Training directly determines how quickly new controllers become available for operational duties. Unit Training is split in two modules in the study: this section focuses on the more general parts of Unit Training, whereas the subsequent section examines the aspects specific to how ATCOs are trained to operate in a given sector, sector-family or cluster.

As presented in Section 4.2, Unit Training was classified as High Incidence and High Severity, making it the only key area to receive a High classification in both indicators. This result indicates that Unit Training is not only one of the most frequently discussed topics across the consultation process but also one of the most consistently reported constraints affecting ANSPs across the European ATM network. The findings therefore identify Unit Training as the principal bottleneck within the ATCO management cycle.

The challenges identified within Unit Training confirm the results of the key area assessment presented in Section 4.2 and explain why this area emerges as the principal bottleneck affecting future ATCO availability. Unlike recruitment or selection, where constraints primarily affect the number of candidates entering the pipeline, Unit Training directly determines how many trainees ultimately become ATCOs.

The most significant challenges relate to instructor availability and the competition between training and operational activities. Stakeholders repeatedly emphasised that training capacity, rather than trainee availability, constitutes the primary constraint on workforce growth. Several ANSPs highlighted that increasing recruitment without simultaneously expanding unit training capacity simply creates larger trainee backlogs rather than producing additional controllers.

2 · Workforce supply

Final Report · Annex F, ATCO Supply

As illustrated in Figure 21, the model captures both the inflows and outflows affecting the operational workforce. The starting point is the number of ATCOs available in the baseline year. This workforce is subsequently adjusted through the addition of newly qualified controllers entering operations and the removal of controllers leaving operational duties.

The principal inflow is the output of the training pipeline. Controllers entering operations in a given year are the result of recruitment decisions taken several years earlier and are therefore subject to both a time delay and an attrition process throughout training. The number of new controllers reaching operations depends on four principal factors: the annual intake of trainees, the duration of the training process, the proportion of trainees successfully completing training, and the overall capacity of the training system. Together, these variables (intake, training duration, success rate & training capacity) determine both the volume and timing of future entries into the operational workforce.

The model also recognises that training throughput is constrained by available resources. Increasing recruitment does not automatically result in a proportional increase in ATCOs if bottlenecks exist elsewhere in the training pipeline. Instructor availability, simulator capacity, OTJ training opportunities and other training resources influence the number of trainees that can progress through the system and ultimately obtain operational endorsements. Training capacity therefore acts as a limiting factor on the production of future controllers.

On the outflow side, the model distinguishes between retirements and other forms of workforce attrition. Retirements are determined by the age structure of the workforce and the retirement provisions applicable to each ANSP. Attrition captures all other departures from operational duties, including resignations, transfers to non-operational functions, medical disqualifications and other forms of permanent withdrawal from operational service. Together, these mechanisms determine the rate at which existing controller capacity exits the system.

3 · Possible measures

Final Report · §4.3, Unit Training solutions

The identified solutions recognise that the principal challenge facing Unit Training is not the availability of trainees, but the limited capacity of the system to qualify them efficiently. Consequently, most measures focus on improving Training Capacity, while a smaller number seek to reduce Training duration and increase the flexibility of training delivery.

Several best practices aim to increase the availability and efficiency of instructor resources. Dedicated management of instructor roles and workloads, coordinating training activities with lower-demand periods, and measures to reduce instructor fatigue all aim to protect training capacity from operational pressures and ensure training activities can continue consistently over time. These approaches directly address the instructor bottlenecks highlighted throughout the consultation process.

A second group of solutions seeks to reduce the operational burden associated with training. Greater use of advanced simulation technologies and more flexible OJT requirements allow trainees to acquire competencies without relying exclusively on live operational environments, thereby reducing the competition between training and service provision. These measures also improve the scalability of training systems and facilitate the expansion of training activity when additional workforce production is required.

The future practices build on the same principles by introducing more fundamental changes to the organisation of training. Competency-based progression models and modular training pathways seek to move away from rigid time-based approaches and allow training programmes to better reflect individual trainee performance. Similarly, expanding the pool of qualified and available instructors and assessors would help alleviate one of the most significant constraints identified across the entire ATCO management cycle.

4 · Future competence

Final Report · Chapter 7, Our proposed solution for future-proofing

Competency-based licensing is an approach in which the scope of an ATCO’s licence privileges is explicitly linked to demonstrated competence across three complementary domains: core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence and operational-environment competence.

The core competence domain defines what an ATCO should be able to do: manage traffic and separation, communicate and coordinate, make decisions, and apply teamwork, self-management and workload management in routine and non-routine situations (building on the core competencies defined under CBTA).

The technical-system competence domain defines which systems and how the ATCO should be able to use: how to operate systems and tools, interpret outputs and understand limitations, supervise automation and respond to degraded or inappropriate system behaviour. This also brings in the perspective of evolving ATM systems and tools, increasing automation levels, and new technologies. These developments can and must be translated into competencies.

The operational-environment competence domain defines the operational environment in which the ATCO needs to operate and how: applying knowledge of the airspace, traffic, and procedures, including local arrangements; anticipating interactions; and adapting to changing configurations and operational complexity. This domain ensures that the local perspective is well defined and is translated into competencies.

Each competence domain comprises competencies built from knowledge, skills, and attitudes, which are explicitly defined and recognised separately, rather than relying on a single unit endorsement as the evidence for the entire bundle. The approach allows interaction between the competence domains but requires that all three domains are maintained, and that none can be completely replaced by competence in the other two domains. This notion is also widely supported by the interviews and stakeholder discussions.
