Executive Summary

Final Report · Executive Summary

Over the last decade, increasing air traffic demand has placed growing pressure on the European Air Traffic Management (ATM) system. In response, a range of operational, technological and regulatory initiatives have been introduced to improve capacity, efficiency and workforce flexibility, including the implementation of Competency-Based Training and Assessment (CBTA). Industry consultations conducted by the Civil Air Navigation Services Organisation (CANSO) also highlighted broad support among European Air Navigation Service Providers (ANSP) for measures aimed at accelerating training throughput, improving training outcomes and increasing workforce flexibility.

Despite these efforts, Air Traffic Controller (ATCO) shortages remain one of the most significant challenges facing the European ATM network. The analysis undertaken in this study shows that, while ATCO availability broadly matched operational requirements until the COVID-19 period, traffic demand recovered more rapidly than workforce growth following the pandemic. As a result, a gap emerged between the number of controllers required to accommodate demand and the number available to provide services.

Figure 1 illustrates this imbalance at the European network level. Under the baseline traffic growth scenario, and assuming ATCO productivity and recruitment remain at current levels, the gap continues to widen throughout the period (as indicated by the blue and green lines). Even under the most favourable yet highly unrealistic hypothetical scenario, where ATCO productivity continues to improve according to the average historical trend, ANSPs manage to significantly increase the rate of bringing in operational controllers, and traffic growth would be lower than what the baseline forecast suggests, the first chance for closing the gap appears in 2035. In practice, persistent workforce pressures are expected to remain across the European ATM network, reinforcing the need for proactive measures to improve workforce sustainability and resilience.

To address this challenge, the study follows a structured analytical approach that progresses from identifying the workforce problem, to quantifying its magnitude, understanding its drivers, investigating its root causes and ultimately identifying the most effective interventions.

The workforce challenge is assessed through the concept of the ATCO gap, defined as the difference between the number of controllers required to accommodate future traffic demand and the number available to provide Air Traffic Services (ATS). The gap is determined by the interaction between demand-side factors, such as traffic volumes and operational productivity, and supply-side factors, including recruitment, training performance, retirements and attrition.

Building on this framework, the study combines quantitative and qualitative evidence to assess both the scale of the workforce challenge and its underlying causes. The quantitative analysis measures the current and projected ATCO gap and evaluates the sensitivity of workforce availability to changes in key variables. The qualitative analysis complements these results by providing operational context, identifying root causes and assessing the practical feasibility of potential solutions.

As shown in Figure 5, the assessment integrates multiple sources of evidence. Baseline data collected from publicly available sources are used to establish the current workforce situation and support the development of a European supply-demand model. This evidence is complemented by a survey completed by 26 ANSPs, which provided both quantitative model inputs and insights into workforce, training and licensing practices. In addition, 17 ANSPs participated in in-depth interviews to provide a deeper understanding of the challenges affecting different stages of the ATCO management cycle and the practical constraints faced by organisations across the network.

The analysis demonstrates that workforce challenges exist across all stages of the ATCO management cycle, but are particularly concentrated within Unit Training, which emerged as the area of greatest concern among participating ANSPs. Constraints related to training capacity, training duration and training success rates were consistently identified as the main bottlenecks limiting the ability of organisations to convert recruitment efforts into operationally qualified controllers. More broadly, the findings confirm that workforce shortages result from multiple interconnected challenges across the ATCO management cycle and therefore require coordinated interventions spanning recruitment, training, licensing and operational deployment.

To identify the most effective responses, the study assessed both existing practices implemented by European ANSPs and future opportunities identified through literature review and stakeholder consultation. In total, 78 solutions were mapped against the workforce challenges they address and the variables influencing the ATCO gap. These solutions were subsequently prioritised according to their importance and implementation feasibility, before being refined through the sensitivity analysis of the supply-demand model. This process reduced the initial longlist to 36 candidate recommendations, representing the measures with the strongest combination of potential impact and practical deliverability.

The prioritisation exercise provides an important insight into the nature of the workforce challenge. Whilst increasing recruitment intake is often perceived as the primary response to ATCO shortages, both stakeholder consultation and modelling results indicate that the principal constraint lies elsewhere. ANSPs consistently highlighted limitations in the training pipeline rather than difficulties in attracting candidates, and the model confirms that improvements in recruitment alone have a comparatively limited impact unless they are accompanied by higher training success rates and a greater capacity to progress trainees towards operational qualification.

As a result, the Training & Licensing pillar emerged as the highest priority area for intervention. Notably, the three highest-ranked recommendations from the ANSP perspective are all associated with this pillar, with two focused on Unit Training, previously identified as the most critical area of the lifecycle, and one on Licensing:

  • Establish dedicated management of instructor roles and workloads;
  • Adopt competency-based progression models; and
  • Facilitate mobility through common operational language arrangements and digital licence management.

Together, these measures aim to increase training effectiveness, improve workforce flexibility and accelerate the transition from trainee to ATCO.

The sensitivity analysis further demonstrates that the variables with the greatest influence on the projected ATCO gap are predominantly operational in nature. Among the controllable factors, ATCO productivity, utilisation and maximum staff hours emerge as the most influential levers, followed by training success rates and recruitment intake. Training capacity and training duration also play a critical enabling role, determining the extent to which improvements in recruitment and training performance can ultimately be translated into additional ATCOs.

The analysis also highlights the scale of the challenge. Under the baseline scenario, eliminating the projected ATCO gap by 2036 would require substantial improvements in the most influential variables, in many cases exceeding 10%. This demonstrates that no single measure is likely to be sufficient and reinforces the need for a coordinated portfolio of interventions addressing multiple stages of the workforce lifecycle.

While ANSPs remain the primary actors responsible for implementing many of the identified measures and must seize every opportunity to address the ATCO shortage, the study also highlights the important role of regulators and technology providers. Several high-impact solutions, particularly those related to licensing, mobility, automation, and advanced operational concepts, require governance and policy support and technology deployment to deliver their full benefits. Reducing future workforce shortages will therefore depend on coordinated action across operational, regulatory and technical stakeholders.

Overall, the study concludes that future ATCO shortages can be mitigated, but not through recruitment measures alone. The greatest opportunity lies in strengthening the efficiency and effectiveness of the training pipeline, increasing workforce flexibility and implementing operational practices and technologies that enhance the productivity and utilisation of qualified controllers.

Conclusions from the analyses

Final Report · Chapter 6, Conclusions from the analyses

The analysis conducted throughout this study confirms that ATCO shortages remain one of the most significant challenges facing the European ATM network. Although the severity of the problem varies between ANSPs and operational environments, the evidence collected demonstrates that workforce shortages are neither temporary nor attributable to a single cause. Instead, they are a structural challenge resulting from the interaction of multiple factors across the entire ATCO management cycle.

The quantitative assessment shows that the imbalance between workforce demand and workforce availability already existed before the COVID-19 pandemic, was temporarily masked by the collapse in traffic demand, and re-emerged rapidly during the subsequent recovery period. The analysis further indicates that this imbalance is likely to persist throughout the coming decade. Even under favourable assumptions regarding recruitment and productivity improvements, workforce pressures remain visible across much of the projection horizon. Although highly optimistic scenarios indicate the gap may eventually close by 2036, this outcome still implies about ten years of sustained workforce constraints, with the period around 2027-2028 appearing particularly critical.

A key finding of the study is that ATCO shortages cannot be explained solely through the current licensing framework. The evidence collected through interviews, surveys and modelling demonstrates that workforce availability is shaped by decisions and constraints occurring throughout the entire ATCO management cycle. Workforce planning, recruitment, selection, training, licensing, operational deployment, workforce mobility, and workforce utilisation all shape the future balance between ATCO demand and supply. Consequently, no single intervention can resolve the challenge in isolation.

Within this broader lifecycle, Unit Training emerged as the most critical area of concern. It was the only key area classified as high in both incidence and severity, reflecting both the frequency with which it was raised by stakeholders and the extent to which it affects ANSPs across the network. Instructor availability, On-The-Job Training capacity and training throughput were consistently identified as the principal bottlenecks limiting the production of new operational ATCOs. More generally, the findings suggest that the key challenge is not attracting candidates into the profession, but rather converting those candidates into operationally qualified ATCOs.

The study also highlights the importance of long-term workforce planning. Given the length of the training pipeline, decisions made today often shape operational capacity years into the future. As a result, workforce shortages frequently reflect historical planning decisions rather than current recruitment activity. This reinforces the need for workforce planning approaches that can anticipate future operational requirements and align recruitment and training capacity accordingly.

The quantitative analysis shows that future workforce requirements depend not only on traffic growth, but also on productivity achieved in the operational environment. Whilst traffic demand remains an important driver, the model shows that productivity assumptions significantly influence the future evolution of workforce demand. Under the scenarios assessed, the most realistic trajectory lies between a situation in which productivity remains broadly unchanged and one in which historical productivity improvements continue over time. The analysis therefore suggests that improvements in operational efficiency will be as important as workforce growth in addressing future shortages.

The sensitivity analysis confirms that not all variables influence the future ATCO gap to the same extent. Among the controllable model variables, ATCO productivity, utilisation and maximum staff hours have the greatest impact on the future workforce balance. On the supply side, training success rates and recruitment intake represent the most influential variables. Training capacity and training duration play a different but equally important role, acting as enabling conditions that determine how improvements in recruitment and training performance can ultimately translate into additional operational ATCOs.

The analysis of mobility and licensing also highlights a key distinction between regulatory flexibility and practical workforce mobility. Whilst stakeholders generally support greater mobility across the European ATM network, the evidence suggests the main barriers are not the licence itself, but the operational realities of local procedures, unit-specific training, airspace characteristics, and language requirements. Improving workforce mobility therefore requires a broader perspective than licensing harmonisation alone.

Taken together, the findings suggest that maintaining current trends will not be sufficient to restore long-term equilibrium between ATCO demand and supply. Active intervention is required, particularly in those parts of the lifecycle that most directly influence the production and deployment of operational ATCOs. The prioritisation exercise confirms this conclusion: the highest-ranked recommendations are concentrated within the Training & Licensing pillar and aim to increase training effectiveness, improve workforce flexibility, and strengthen the transition from trainee to ATCO.

In particular, three measures consistently emerged as the most promising interventions:

  • Establish dedicated management of instructor roles and workloads ;
  • Adopt competency-based progression models; and
  • Facilitate mobility through common operational language arrangements and digital licence management.

These recommendations reflect a broader conclusion of the study: future ATCO shortages will not be resolved through recruitment measures alone. The greatest opportunity lies in strengthening the efficiency of the training pipeline, increasing workforce flexibility and improving the operational utilisation of qualified ATCOs.

Finally, the study highlights that addressing the workforce challenge requires coordinated action across the ATM ecosystem. ANSPs remain the primary actors responsible for implementing many of the identified measures, but regulators and technology providers also play a critical role in enabling improvements related to licensing, mobility, automation and future operational concepts. Long-term workforce sustainability will therefore depend on a combination of organisational, regulatory and technological initiatives working towards a common objective: ensuring that the European ATM network can continue to provide safe, efficient and resilient air traffic services in the face of future demand.
