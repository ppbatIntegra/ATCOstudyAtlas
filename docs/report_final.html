The workforce challenge and response

Final Report · Executive Summary

Over the last decade, increasing air traffic demand has placed growing pressure on the European Air Traffic Management (ATM) system. In response, a range of operational, technological and regulatory initiatives have been introduced to improve capacity, efficiency and workforce flexibility, including the implementation of Competency-Based Training and Assessment (CBTA). Industry consultations conducted by the Civil Air Navigation Services Organisation (CANSO) also highlighted broad support among European Air Navigation Service Providers (ANSP) for measures aimed at accelerating training throughput, improving training outcomes and increasing workforce flexibility.

Despite these efforts, Air Traffic Controller (ATCO) shortages remain one of the most significant challenges facing the European ATM network. The analysis undertaken in this study shows that, while ATCO availability broadly matched operational requirements until the COVID-19 period, traffic demand recovered more rapidly than workforce growth following the pandemic. As a result, a gap emerged between the number of controllers required to accommodate demand and the number available to provide services.

ATCO shortages have both short- and long-term aspects. On the one hand, ANSPs need to close the ATCO gap as soon as possible, which might also require short-term regulatory actions at various levels. On the other hand, beyond the end of the decade and further ahead, new operational concepts and ATM technologies will be implemented, and overall ATCO training and licensing schemes must enable and support these changes and the transition. This requires long-term regulatory action at the European level. The study investigates both of these aspects.

The analysis demonstrates that workforce challenges exist across all stages of the ATCO management cycle, but are particularly concentrated within Unit Training, which emerged as the area of greatest concern among participating ANSPs. Constraints related to training capacity, training duration and training success rates were consistently identified as the main bottlenecks limiting the ability of organisations to convert recruitment efforts into operationally qualified controllers. More broadly, the findings confirm that workforce shortages result from multiple interconnected challenges across the ATCO management cycle and therefore require coordinated interventions spanning recruitment, training, licensing and operational deployment.

To identify the most effective responses, the study assessed both existing practices implemented by European ANSPs and future opportunities identified through literature review and stakeholder consultation. In total, 78 solutions were mapped against the workforce challenges they address and the variables influencing the ATCO gap. These solutions were subsequently prioritised according to their importance and implementation feasibility, before being refined through the sensitivity analysis of the supply-demand model. This process reduced the initial longlist to 36 candidate recommendations, representing the measures with the strongest combination of potential impact and practical deliverability.

The prioritisation exercise provides an important insight into the nature of the workforce challenge. Whilst increasing recruitment intake is often perceived as the primary response to ATCO shortages, both stakeholder consultation and modelling results indicate that the principal constraint lies elsewhere. ANSPs consistently highlighted limitations in the training process rather than difficulties in attracting candidates, and the model confirms that improvements in recruitment alone have a comparatively limited impact unless they are accompanied by higher training success rates and a greater capacity to progress trainees towards operational qualification.

As a result, the Training & Licensing pillar emerged as the highest priority area for intervention. Notably, the three highest-ranked recommendations from the ANSP perspective are all associated with this pillar, with two focused on Unit Training, previously identified as the most critical area of the lifecycle, and one on Licensing:

  • Establish dedicated management of instructor roles and workloads;
  • Adopt competency-based progression models; and
  • Facilitate mobility through common operational language arrangements and digital licence management.

Together, these measures aim to increase training effectiveness, improve workforce flexibility and accelerate the transition from trainee to ATCO.

The sensitivity analysis further demonstrates that the variables with the greatest influence on the projected ATCO gap are predominantly operational in nature. Among the controllable factors, ATCO productivity, utilisation and maximum staff hours emerge as the most influential levers, followed by training success rates and recruitment intake. Training capacity and training duration also play a critical enabling role, determining the extent to which improvements in recruitment and training performance can ultimately be translated into additional ATCOs.

The analysis also highlights the scale of the challenge. Under the baseline scenario, eliminating the projected ATCO gap by 2036 would require substantial improvements in the most influential variables, in many cases exceeding 10%. This demonstrates that no single measure is likely to be sufficient and reinforces the need for a coordinated portfolio of interventions addressing multiple stages of the workforce lifecycle.

While ANSPs remain the primary actors responsible for implementing many of the identified measures and must seize every opportunity to address the ATCO shortage, the study also highlights the important role of regulators and technology providers. Several high-impact solutions, particularly those related to licensing, mobility, automation, and advanced operational concepts, require governance and policy support and technology deployment to deliver their full benefits. Reducing future workforce shortages will therefore depend on coordinated action across operational, regulatory and technical stakeholders.

Overall, the study concludes that future ATCO shortages can be mitigated, but not through recruitment measures alone. The greatest opportunity lies in strengthening the efficiency and effectiveness of the training process, increasing workforce flexibility and implementing operational practices and technologies that enhance the productivity and utilisation of qualified controllers.

Future-proofing training and licensing

Final Report · Executive Summary

Automation, new ATM systems, cross-border operations, and evolving service-delivery models increasingly change what controllers do and how responsibilities are shared between humans and systems. The current training and licensing framework relies on ratings and unit endorsements that bundle together a wide range of capabilities. This limits transparency on which competencies are transferable and can make mobility and requalification more burdensome than necessary.

The study addresses this by developing a task- and competency-based analytical framework for the ATCO role. It examines how automation changes individual ATCO tasks, separates aptitude, knowledge, skills and attitudes, competence, demonstrated performance and formal qualification, and identifies which elements of competence are generic and which depend on a specific technical or operational environment. A key contribution of the study is the resulting three-domain model of core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence and operational-environment competence, providing a structured basis for analysing transferability, training needs and qualification gaps.

Building on this analysis, the study develops a competency-based licensing (CBL) concept in which qualification focuses more directly on what an ATCO can demonstrably do, in which technical environment and under which operational conditions. Rather than treating a change of unit or environment as requiring a largely complete requalification pathway, the concept enables the recognition of already demonstrated competencies and focuses training and assessment on the remaining gaps. This provides a basis for more modular, targeted training; greater mobility and cross-border deployment; better recognition of prior competence; and a licensing framework that can evolve with technology and automation while maintaining the required level of operational safety.

Findings, outcomes, and key tools from the study

While DG MOVE commissioned the study and one objective focused on the ATCO training and licensing framework, introducing new regulation in the short term was not necessarily foreseen as an outcome. Indeed, the study confirmed ample flexibility in the existing regulatory framework: We found highly divergent interpretations and applications of the common, harmonised rules, and while stakeholders almost never mentioned regulation as an obstacle, some sought more guidance on its interpretation options. It appeared that while flexibility exists, a lack of confidence in the framework or in the appropriateness of the interpretation is hindering its use.

The backbone of the study's evidence base was information collected through interviews and survey responses from ANSPs and other stakeholders. This shows strong engagement across the European ATM community to address the ATCO shortage. This engagement needs to translate into a willingness to act and cooperate to identify and implement the necessary measures.

The study found many solutions and good practices already implemented by some ANSPs that successfully addressed certain issues. These solutions are distributed across the entire ATCO pipeline and relate to, e.g., flexibility in the training process, certain selection practices, flexible and optimised rostering schemes, self-practice opportunities for students, etc. We encourage ANSPs to look into all solutions listed in the study and examine how they can be implemented in various environments. This also highlights the importance of regular, structured and focused dialogue between ANSPs and all stakeholders.

While the study does not directly propose new regulations, it does provide a dedicated perspective on solutions that may require governance actions at various levels (i.e., national, European, etc.). Some of the solutions and concepts from the study will also serve as input for EASA’s rulemaking tasks, which are defined by EASA’s European Plan for Aviation Safety (EPAS).

Finally, we highlight the three most important concepts defined in the study:

  • The notion of the ATCO gap, its urgency, its severity, and its foreseeable evolution, as indicated in our quantitative models,
  • The concept of the ATCO pipeline, which covers the entire management cycle from the strategic planning of resources, through training, to operations and beyond,
  • And Competency-based Licensing, which is a concept that is building on the learnings from CBTA and may offer a way to make the ATCO training and licensing scheme future proof.

Recommendations

Final Report · Executive Summary · Recommendations

Based on the analyses, findings, and conclusions of the study, we invite the various stakeholders of the European ATM community to consider the following recommendations.

  • ANSPs should explore the root-cause analysis and the solution catalogue to identify practices applicable to them, identify the most effective local measures for closing the ATCO gap, and implement them as soon as practically possible.
  • ANSPs should also engage in regular, structured discussions around the topics addressed in the study to share experience, knowledge, and practices, and to ensure that the community can learn from individual efforts and ideas.
  • NSAs, ANSPs, PSOs and other state-level stakeholders involved in the planning of ANSP staffing and ATCO training and licensing should engage in regular, structured discussions around the topics addressed in the study and define ways of addressing the local ATCO gap as early as practically possible, and to ensure that all stakeholders share an understanding of the urgency of the issue, the underlying locally specific root causes, and the possible solutions.
  • The European Commission should support regular, structured discussions within the ATM community and among the state-level stakeholders involved in staffing, training and licensing. This could involve providing suitable platforms or encouraging better use of existing ones.
  • The European Commission, together with EASA, and the Network Manager, and operational stakeholders, is recommended to define ways for monitoring the actions taken and solutions implemented by ANSPs and other relevant stakeholders in addressing ATCO shortages, and to follow the evolution of the ATCO gap regularly.
  • The European Commission is recommended to consider competency-based licensing as the potential future architecture for ATCO licensing. EASA is recommended to consider competency-based licensing in the context of its RMT.0738.
  • EASA is recommended to consider findings from the study related to the ATCO training licensing framework in the context of its RMT.0668-5.
  • The European Commission, EASA, Member States, and NCAs/NSAs are invited to explore, especially the governance view of, the solution catalogue and identify items where they could support addressing the issues with governance, policy, and regulatory actions.
  • The European Commission, the SESAR 3 Joint Undertaking, and the SESAR Deployment Manager are invited to explore the availability of technical developments enabling the solutions indicated in this report. To this end, the institutions involved should define the development, industrialisation and deployment paths leading to further efficiencies and availability of more powerful tools for ATCOs.
  • We recommend that the European Commission consider the study's findings and examine how the performance and charging scheme of the SES may support Member States and ANSPs in addressing existing issues, and how the findings may be taken forward in the context of RP5 preparations.
