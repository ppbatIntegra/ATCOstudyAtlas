Knowledge, skills, attitudes, competence and performance

Final Report · Chapter 7, Understanding the relevance of competence

The immediate outputs of learning can be expressed in terms of knowledge, skills and attitudes (KSA). These are the resources that an individual acquires and draws upon when performing operational tasks, but they do not in themselves constitute competence. Knowledge may include factual and conceptual knowledge, procedures, system knowledge, operational knowledge and knowledge of the local operating environment. Skills may include cognitive and perceptual skills, procedural and technical skills, HMI and system-operation skills, communication, coordination and interpersonal skills. Attitudes concern the manner in which these capabilities are applied, including safety orientation, professionalism, responsibility, willingness to cooperate, openness to feedback, adaptability and commitment to continued development. Competent performance requires mobilising these elements together rather than applying them in isolation. This distinction is consistent with the EU CBTA definition of competency, which explicitly refers to behaviours that mobilise the relevant knowledge, skills and attitudes in carrying out activities or tasks under specified conditions.

Competence represents the integrated capability to perform the activities required by the job successfully. It is therefore more than possessing relevant knowledge or being proficient in a particular skill. A competent ATCO must be able to select, combine and apply the appropriate knowledge, skills and attitudes in response to the task and the conditions in which it is performed. Competence answers the question of what the individual is capable of doing. The EU CBTA framework defines competency as a dimension of human performance that predicts successful job performance and is manifested through observable behaviours that mobilise relevant knowledge, skills and attitudes.

Performance is the observable demonstration of competence. Whereas competence describes an integrated capability, performance provides the evidence from which achievement of that capability can be assessed. It must therefore be considered in relation to the task being performed and the operational and technical conditions under which the performance is observed. Relevant evidence includes observable behaviours, consistency of performance, the integration of different competencies and the operational outcome achieved. Under the EU CBTA framework, an assessment determines whether a person meets the required performance level under given conditions by collecting evidence from observable behaviours, while performance criteria combine an observable behaviour, one or more conditions and a competency standard. This establishes a direct relationship between competence, observable behaviour, conditions and the performance standard against which competence is assessed.

The final layer, qualification and licensing, represents the formal recognition that specified requirements have been met and that the individual is authorised to exercise defined privileges. This is a regulatory or administrative status rather than another human capability. Under the present European ATCO licensing framework, formal recognition is expressed through the ATCO or student ATCO licence and, as applicable, ratings, rating endorsements, unit endorsements, language endorsements and instructor or assessor endorsements. Medical fitness constitutes a separate prerequisite for the exercise of operational privileges and should therefore not be conflated with competence itself. The licensing layer therefore answers a different question from the preceding stages: not what the individual knows or can do, but which capabilities and qualifications have been formally recognised and which privileges the individual is authorised to exercise.

Pillars and key areas

Final Report · Annex G, Definitions of pillars and key areas

Table 41: Pillars and key areas of the ATCO management cycle (Source: Consortium assessment)

Key areasDefinition
Transversal
The structural and external conditions that shape every stage of the lifecycle rather than any single one. They set the flexibility available to a ANSP before any specific decision is taken.
ANSP factorsThe institutional and organisational characteristics of each ANSP, including ownership structure, governance arrangements, budgetary autonomy and employment frameworks. These factors establish the organisational context within which workforce-related decisions are taken.
Exogenous factorsThe external conditions influencing workforce planning, training and operational delivery that remain outside the direct control of ANSPs. These include traffic developments, national legislation, labour market conditions and regulatory or technological change.
Strategic planningThe processes through which ANSPs forecast future controller requirements and translate those forecasts into workforce, recruitment and training plans. It includes planning horizons, forecasting methodologies and the alignment between workforce needs and training capacity.
Hiring
The process leading from the definition of future workforce needs to the admission of a candidate into the training pipeline, together with the employment conditions that determine whether qualified controllers are retained.
Working conditionsThe employment conditions and workforce management practices applied to ATCOs throughout their careers. This includes remuneration, career development, rostering flexibility, retirement arrangements and other factors influencing workforce attraction and retention.
RecruitmentThe processes through which ANSPs attract potential candidates to the ATCO profession and encourage them to apply. It includes recruitment campaigns, eligibility requirements, employer attractiveness and access to potential candidate pools.
SelectionThe processes used to assess applicants and determine their suitability for entry into the ATCO training pipeline. It includes aptitude assessments, behavioural evaluation, medical certification and other selection mechanisms intended to identify candidates most likely to succeed during training.
Training & Licensing
The process that turns a selected candidate into a licensed controller authorised to operate at a given unit. It governs both the throughput of the pipeline and the flexibility with which licensed controllers can later be deployed.
Initial training (Theory)The theoretical training phase through which trainees acquire the knowledge and competencies required before progressing to practical and simulator-based activities.
Initial training (SIM)The simulator-based training phase through which trainees begin applying theoretical knowledge in a controlled operational environment. It includes simulator availability, exercise design, instructor resources and practical training methodologies.
Unit trainingThe unit-specific training required before a controller can operate independently within a particular operational environment. It includes transitional training, simulator activities, on-the-job training and the associated instructor and assessor resources.
Sector specific trainingThe training and endorsement processes through which controllers acquire and maintain competence for specific sectors or sector groups within a unit. It includes endorsement structures, currency requirements and cross-sector qualification arrangements.
LicensingThe processes governing the issue, maintenance, recognition and transfer of ATCO licences, ratings and endorsements. It includes the implementation of European licensing requirements and their implications for operational flexibility and controller mobility.
Operational
The deployment of available controller capacity in the operating environment, and the use of operational and training outcomes to improve the earlier stages of the lifecycle.
RosteringThe planning and allocation of available controller resources to operational duties. It includes roster design, shift allocation, working-time arrangements and the management of operational availability.
OperationsThe operational utilisation of available controller resources to provide air traffic services. It includes sector configuration, airspace design, supporting technologies, automation and other factors influencing operational productivity and capacity.
Post-OPS analysisThe collection and analysis of operational and training performance information to support continuous improvement across the ATCO management cycle. It includes the monitoring of outcomes, identification of lessons learned and feedback mechanisms for workforce planning, selection and training.
