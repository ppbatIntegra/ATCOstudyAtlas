Applications of competency-based licensing

The Final Report illustrates CBL through recognition between units, cross-unit delegation and the competence needed to use a conflict-detection tool. These are potential applications of the proposed concept.

Recognising competence when moving between units

Final Report · §7.6 · Licensing benefits

Arguably, the biggest licensing benefit of the CBL concept comes from its modular approach, which turns binary unit endorsement into a more nuanced and flexible exercise. This is achieved first by moving the focus from the ATS unit to the competences required by ATS units in the three domains, making unit endorsements comparable. When CBL is implemented, even if unit endorsements are retained, endorsements for specific units would rely on the qualifications in each competence domain, meaning the performance standards and observable behaviours per competency required for each unit would provide a structured, transparent means of comparison.

Not only would this allow for comparison of different unit requirements, but it would also allow comparison of an ATCO’s licence privileges with the requirements of a given unit. This would open up the possibility of recognising and accepting the qualifications an ATCO has achieved that meet the unit requirements, and only requiring training and a qualification for the gap. We understand this approach would be significantly more flexible while also being more structured and harmonised than current practice.

Cross-unit and cross-border delegation

Final Report · §7.6 · Licensing benefits

As a specific use-case, we also highlight that more dynamic cross-border or cross-unit airspace delegation solutions may also benefit significantly from this approach, especially if the technical-system domain remains largely stable during the delegation (meaning that ATCOs receiving the delegation use their “own” ATM system environment to provide the service). In such cases, cross-training and then endorsement could focus on the missing qualifications in the operational-environment domain (assuming the same requirements in the core competence domain, of course).

A technical-system competence example

Final Report · §7.4 · Building blocks of unit competence

Knowledge, skills and attitudes are already required to operate the technical environment, including ATM systems, communication tools and controller interfaces. A conflict-detection tool illustrates how these elements combine into competence.

  • Understanding how a conflict-detection tool works, what the look-ahead time is, and its parameters and key assumptions is knowledge.
  • Interpreting alerts, distinguishing relevant from false alerts, and querying trajectories is a skill.
  • Having appropriate trust and vigilance toward the tool, and retaining ownership and accountability for decisions based on the tool's information, is an attitude.
  • Maintaining separation, resolving conflicts safely, and using the conflict-detection tool effectively and appropriately is competence.

Similar examples can be developed for many other elements of the technical environment ATCOs need to navigate while performing their tasks. This shows that, rather than a single cross-cutting competency, ATCOs require an entire competence domain to perform their duties safely and efficiently in their respective technical environments.

We can also apply the same logic to working in the local operational environment element of the unit competence bundle. ATCOs need to know local Letters of Agreement (LoAs) that set out agreed coordination procedures, know the traffic patterns and anticipate conflict points, have the skills to sequence traffic efficiently in the local structure, and have a proactive attitude towards coordination. These knowledge, skill, and attitude elements combine into several competencies that together comprise the operational-environment competence domain.
