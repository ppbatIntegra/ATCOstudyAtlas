From operational change to tasks

Final Report · §7.1 · Future changes

The first step in our analysis was to examine how future ATM tools, operational concepts, and general technical evolution affect air traffic controllers’ work. To this end, we analysed seven of the key emerging operational concepts from the SESAR framework:

  • Trajectory-based Operations (TBO)
  • Advanced Flexible Use of Airspace (A-FUA) and Dynamic Airspace Configuration (DAC)
  • Complex CPDLC clearances
  • Remote Tower Operations (including digital towers)
  • Advanced conflict detection and automated resolution support
  • Automatic speech recognition and attention guidance
  • AI-based ATC digital assistants.

These seven concepts represent important elements of the SESAR Master Plan or emerging technical changes, including AI. They provide a starting point for examining how technology may change ATCO tasks.

The concepts can also be read as a progression in the functions assigned to automation, from information handling towards wider operational action. The categories below are analytical rather than formal SESAR capability levels.

Across these examples, we can observe an increased reliance on automation, which progressively supports or performs components of tasks historically carried out by controllers: information acquisition, situation prediction, solution generation, coordination and, at higher levels, action implementation. This suggests a systematic change in the balance between direct task execution and the review, supervision and intervention activities associated with automated systems.

This direction is explicit in the European ATM Master Plan (2025 edition). Its Automation Roadmap envisages ATM at automation level 2 by 2035 and, for certain flight phases and within a predefined operating scope, level 4 by 2045. These are strategic targets, not descriptions of today’s operational baseline. The roadmap’s levels distinguish decision support, resolution support, conditional automation and confined automation for examining task redistribution.

Understanding these changes requires analysis of individual tasks and how they may be redistributed between controllers and automation. The task taxonomy and human-role analysis provides that level of detail.

Task analysis and competence

Final Report · §7.2 · Generic task taxonomy

The analysis of SESAR concepts and the Automation Roadmap gave us insight into how technology and automation can affect different tasks. The Automation Roadmap also introduced the notion of Levels of Automation (LoAT), a logical framework for analysing how authority is allocated between humans and automation.

The LoAT framework breaks down each task into four distinct information processing phases:

  • information acquisition;
  • information analysis;
  • decision/action selection;
  • action implementation.

If we use the example of conflict management as an ATCO task, these stages roughly correspond to the following task elements:

  • Conflict detection,
  • Conflict assessment and measurements,
  • Solution generation,
  • Solution selection/decision,
  • Issue instruction/clearance/intervention,
  • Monitor execution.

Combining the task-level breakdown with LoAT logic, we created a list of generic ATCO tasks that is broad enough to cover most use cases and ATS units, yet specific enough to enable analysis of the changes. This generic ATCO task taxonomy is built on work done previously by ICAO and Eurocontrol, and comprises 34 generic tasks across four areas:

  • Information acquisition, situational awareness and workload,
  • Conflict detection, separation, and traffic management,
  • Clearances, monitoring, communication and coordination, and
  • Local operations, non-routine situations, automation and supporting tasks.

The study developed its 34-task taxonomy from EUROCONTROL’s Integrated Task and Job Analysis of Air Traffic Controllers and the Episode 3 Today’s Operations Task Analysis. It cross-checked the result against ICAO Doc 10056-1 (2022), including example observable behaviours. Additional study-level generalisations address non-routine operations and automation. The taxonomy is an analytical framework for this study. Its source mapping is recorded in generic task taxonomy of the Final Report.

We used the task taxonomy to map tasks to core ATCO competencies (using the existing ICAO/EASA competency reference), describe current human roles, estimate automation potential, and assess how the human role would evolve if full automation potential were realised. We then completed the analysis with also assessing the possible changes in the required competencies as well.

We described the human role using a classification derived from the LoAT logic, focusing on the residual human role. In this approach, the human can “Perform” a task, “Approve” the solution provided by automation, “Supervise” the automated system as it performs a task, “Intervene” and take over when the system is not performing up to the expected standards and functionality, or be “Non-critical/not relevant anymore”, meaning that the residual human role is not relevant from a qualification or licensing perspective. This classification is referred to as the P/A/S/I/N logic.

We performed the analysis to explore the future effects of increasing automation; therefore, the results are indicative only and should be tested against the relevant operational concepts and safety cases before any further use outside this study.

From competence to licensing

Final Report · §7.5 · CBL

Combining everything we learned from the analyses, we developed the concept of Competency-Based Licensing (CBL), built on the three competence domains, and defined it as follows.

Competency-based licensing is an approach in which the scope of an ATCO’s licence privileges is explicitly linked to demonstrated competence across three complementary domains: core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence and operational-environment competence.

The core competence domain defines what an ATCO should be able to do: manage traffic and separation, communicate and coordinate, make decisions, and apply teamwork, self-management and workload management in routine and non-routine situations (building on the core competencies defined under CBTA).

The technical-system competence domain defines which systems and how the ATCO should be able to use: how to operate systems and tools, interpret outputs and understand limitations, supervise automation and respond to degraded or inappropriate system behaviour. This also brings in the perspective of evolving ATM systems and tools, increasing automation levels, and new technologies. These developments can and must be translated into competencies.

The operational-environment competence domain defines the operational environment in which the ATCO needs to operate and how: applying knowledge of the airspace, traffic, and procedures, including local arrangements; anticipating interactions; and adapting to changing configurations and operational complexity. This domain ensures that the local perspective is well defined and is translated into competencies.

Each competence domain comprises competencies built from knowledge, skills, and attitudes, which are explicitly defined and recognised separately, rather than relying on a single unit endorsement as the evidence for the entire bundle. The approach allows interaction between the competence domains but requires that all three domains are maintained, and that none can be completely replaced by competence in the other two domains. This notion is also widely supported by the interviews and stakeholder discussions.

CBL builds on CBTA and draws on system-based licensing while retaining the operational-environment competence domain. Advanced system functionality continues to require competence in the operational setting.

The existing architecture of ATCO licensing can be described as the combination of the rating, the rating endorsements where applicable, and the unit endorsement (and potentially additional endorsements). A conceptual future licence architecture based on CBL could consist of the core competence qualification, the technical-system qualification or endorsement, and the operational-environment qualification or endorsement. This would mean a shift in licensing towards relying on explicitly demonstrated competency components rather than primarily using the named ATS unit as the proxy for competence.
