Future operational and technical changes

Final Report · Chapter 7, How to future-proof?

While the above description of future-proofing sounds relatively straightforward, getting there is anything but. To make it manageable, we break it down into two phases:

  • First, we look at how the future for ATM and ATCOs in particular can be defined in more detail;
  • Second, we look at the proofing part and offer a solution we believe could be the way forward.

When defining the future for ATM and ATCOs, we examine new technologies and operational concepts, understand how they change ATCOs' jobs, and translate that shift into changes in the required skills, knowledge, and competences.

When looking at the proofing part, we seek a way forward that accommodates change while maintaining safety through clear requirements and demonstrable performance, preserving credible oversight via competent, evidence-based assurance, avoiding unnecessary administrative burden, and devising stable rules with adaptable details.

With the logical frame and success criteria defined, the following sections provide an overview of the analyses and the proposed approach to future-proofing the ATCO licensing framework.

Future operational and technical changes

The first step in our analysis was to examine how future ATM tools, operational concepts, and general technical evolution affect air traffic controllers’ work. To this end, we analysed seven of the key emerging operational concepts from the SESAR framework:

  • Trajectory-based Operations (TBO)
  • Advanced Flexible Use of Airspace (A-FUA) and Dynamic Airspace Configuration (DAC)
  • Complex CPDLC clearances
  • Remote Tower Operations (including digital towers)
  • Advanced conflict detection and automated resolution support
  • Automatic speech recognition and attention guidance
  • AI-based ATC digital assistants.

We selected these concepts, as they either represent central elements of the Masterplan or are key technical transformations and introduce emerging technologies (such as AI). In any case, the list is not meant to be exclusive and serves only as a starting point for the analysis. Task analysis and automation

Final Report · Chapter 7, Generic task taxonomy

The analysis of SESAR concepts and the Automation Roadmap gave us insight into how technology and automation can affect different tasks. The Automation Roadmap also introduced the notion of Levels of Automation (LoAT), a logical framework for analysing how authority is allocated between humans and automation.

The LoAT framework breaks down each task into four distinct information processing phases:

  • information acquisition;
  • information analysis;
  • decision/action selection;
  • action implementation.

If we use the example of conflict management as an ATCO task, these stages roughly correspond to the following task elements:

  • Conflict detection,
  • Conflict assessment and measurements,
  • Solution generation,
  • Solution selection/decision,
  • Issue instruction/clearance/intervention,
  • Monitor execution.

Combining the task-level breakdown with LoAT logic, we created a list of generic ATCO tasks that is broad enough to cover most use cases and ATS units, yet specific enough to enable analysis of the changes. This generic ATCO task taxonomy is built on work done previously by ICAO and Eurocontrol, and comprises 34 generic tasks across four areas:

  • Information acquisition, situational awareness and workload,
  • Conflict detection, separation, and traffic management,
  • Clearances, monitoring, communication and coordination, and
  • Local operations, non-routine situations, automation and supporting tasks.

The generalised ATCO task taxonomy was developed by synthesising the EUROCONTROL Integrated Task and Job Analysis of Air Traffic Controllers (Phases 2 and 3) and the Episode 3 Today's Operations Task Analysis. These sources were used to identify and consolidate the principal operational and cognitive activities of air traffic controllers across different working environments. The resulting taxonomy was cross-checked against ICAO Doc 10056-1 (2022), particularly its competency-based training and assessment framework and example observable behaviours. The study introduced additional study-level generalisations to accommodate non-routine operations, automation-related functions, and potential future shifts in task distribution between human controllers and technical systems. The resulting taxonomy is an analytical framework developed for the study, rather than a formally prescribed ICAO or EUROCONTROL classification. The full list of the generic tasks and the mapping with the relevant source documents is provided in Annex M.

We used the task taxonomy to map tasks to core ATCO competencies (using the existing ICAO/EASA competency reference), describe current human roles, estimate automation potential, and assess how the human role would evolve if full automation potential were realised. We then completed the analysis with also assessing the possible changes in the required competencies as well.

We described the human role using a classification derived from the LoAT logic, focusing on the residual human role. In this approach, the human can “Perform” a task, “Approve” the solution provided by automation, “Supervise” the automated system as it performs a task, “Intervene” and take over when the system is not performing up to the expected standards and functionality, or be “Non-critical/not relevant anymore”, meaning that the residual human role is not relevant from a qualification or licensing perspective. This classification is referred to as the P/A/S/I/N logic.

We performed the analysis to explore the future effects of increasing automation; therefore, the results are indicative only and should be tested against the relevant operational concepts and safety cases before any further use outside this study.

From tasks to competence

Final Report · Chapter 7, Understanding the relevance of competence

The analysis of the ATCO task taxonomy shows how automation may affect individual tasks in the future, and how those changes transfer to competency requirements, to the extent that new competencies may need to be introduced. Before moving forward with the future-proofing analysis, we establish a layer model to fully understand how competences fit into the ATCO training and licensing logic.

Taken together, the model illustrates a progression from potential, through development and acquisition, to integrated capability, observable demonstration, and finally formal recognition. It is particularly useful for the present study because it allows recruitment, training, competency development, assessment and licensing to be considered as related but distinct elements of the ATCO qualification pathway. It also provides a basis for examining whether future licensing arrangements could recognise competence more explicitly, rather than relying primarily on the completion of predefined training pathways or unit-specific qualification structures.
