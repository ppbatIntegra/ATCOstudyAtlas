Unpacking unit competence

Final Report · §7.4 · Building blocks of unit competence

Breaking unit competence and endorsements into building blocks helps us future-proof the framework. Under the current framework, unit competence can be described as the bundle of the following key building blocks:

  • The core ATCO competences (defined under CBTA),
  • The technical environment in which the ATCOs operate, including the ATM systems, the controller working positions (CWP), the human-machine interface(s) (HMI), the tools the ATCOs have access to, etc., and the ATCO’s ability to navigate this environment,
  • The local operational environment, including traffic patterns, density, seasonality, operational procedures, airspace structures, local rules and regulations, organisational arrangements, etc., and the ATCO’s ability to operate in this environment,
  • The Language requirements.

Unit endorsement currently combines core competence, technical and operational environments, and language requirements within a geographical area of responsibility and an organisational unit. The licensing and recognition page considers how a more explicit competence structure could improve transparency.

Knowledge, skills and attitudes are already required to operate the technical environment, including ATM systems, communication tools and controller interfaces. A conflict-detection tool illustrates how these elements combine into competence.

  • Understanding how a conflict-detection tool works, what the look-ahead time is, and its parameters and key assumptions is knowledge.
  • Interpreting alerts, distinguishing relevant from false alerts, and querying trajectories is a skill.
  • Having appropriate trust and vigilance toward the tool, and retaining ownership and accountability for decisions based on the tool's information, is an attitude.
  • Maintaining separation, resolving conflicts safely, and using the conflict-detection tool effectively and appropriately is competence.

Similar examples can be developed for many other elements of the technical environment ATCOs need to navigate while performing their tasks. This shows that, rather than a single cross-cutting competency, ATCOs require an entire competence domain to perform their duties safely and efficiently in their respective technical environments.

We can also apply the same logic to working in the local operational environment element of the unit competence bundle. ATCOs need to know local Letters of Agreement (LoAs) that set out agreed coordination procedures, know the traffic patterns and anticipate conflict points, have the skills to sequence traffic efficiently in the local structure, and have a proactive attitude towards coordination. These knowledge, skill, and attitude elements combine into several competencies that together comprise the operational-environment competence domain.

Under the current framework, and with the introduction of the CBTA logic, core competences are spelt out in detail, while the technical and local operational environments are typically handled at the level of operational conditions and standards. But what if we elevated the technical-system competence domain and the operational-environment competence domain to the same level as the core competences and focused the endorsement on those?

The three competence domains

Final Report · §7.5 · Competency-based licensing

Combining everything we learned from the analyses, we developed the concept of Competency-Based Licensing (CBL), built on the three competence domains, and defined it as follows.

Competency-based licensing is an approach in which the scope of an ATCO’s licence privileges is explicitly linked to demonstrated competence across three complementary domains: core ATCO competencies, technical-system competence and operational-environment competence.

The core competence domain defines what an ATCO should be able to do: manage traffic and separation, communicate and coordinate, make decisions, and apply teamwork, self-management and workload management in routine and non-routine situations (building on the core competencies defined under CBTA).

The technical-system competence domain defines which systems and how the ATCO should be able to use: how to operate systems and tools, interpret outputs and understand limitations, supervise automation and respond to degraded or inappropriate system behaviour. This also brings in the perspective of evolving ATM systems and tools, increasing automation levels, and new technologies. These developments can and must be translated into competencies.

The operational-environment competence domain defines the operational environment in which the ATCO needs to operate and how: applying knowledge of the airspace, traffic, and procedures, including local arrangements; anticipating interactions; and adapting to changing configurations and operational complexity. This domain ensures that the local perspective is well defined and is translated into competencies.

Each competence domain comprises competencies built from knowledge, skills, and attitudes, which are explicitly defined and recognised separately, rather than relying on a single unit endorsement as the evidence for the entire bundle. The approach allows interaction between the competence domains but requires that all three domains are maintained, and that none can be completely replaced by competence in the other two domains. This notion is also widely supported by the interviews and stakeholder discussions.
